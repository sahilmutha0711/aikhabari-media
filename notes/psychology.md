# Scroll psychology & retention notes (living file)
Read by every script run (playbook §00d). A daily scheduled research task appends new dated findings at the bottom.
Keep the "CURRENT TOOLKIT" section short (max 15 bullets) and up to date; log raw findings in the dated log.

## CURRENT TOOLKIT (use in every script)
1. Autopilot breaker: the first line must contradict a belief the viewer holds, or reveal a stake for THEM ("Everyone on H-1B thinks they get 60 days... A new rule wants to make it zero."). Say it as a statement, not a question: question headlines tested worse on credibility and engagement and vague clickbait did not beat plain headlines (log 11-07).
2. Social currency and emotion: give one fact their friends don't know yet, or a ready-made opinion. Name the one emotion the Reel leaves: awe, justified anger, anxiety WITH an action, or amusement. Never end sad: in research sadness lowers sharing (our Canva layoffs Reel: 3 saves, 0 shares, though length also differed). Negative stake words help the stop only a little; the send comes from a usable line (log 11-09).
3. Re-commit beat within 3-6 s of the hook: a second reason to stay ("Watch till the end, number 3 is the one nobody's talking about" / "Save this, you'll need it on Nov 10").
4. Open loops every 8-10 s: numbered items ("rule two... rule three is the quiet one"), "and that's not even the worst part", a question you answer later. Make each gap specific and about something the viewer half-knows (visa days, salaries, a famous company), where a guess feels 50/50; that is where curiosity peaks. A "guess before I say it" beat with a short pause, then the answer, is the strongest form. Close every loop you open.
5. Pattern interrupt every 2-3 s on screen (new visual, number pop, cut) and a tone change in the voice at the rehook. Voice: energy over speed. Keep the natural fast pace (about 3 words/s, 165-190 wpm), never speed the voice up in editing, and slow down with a ~1 s pause only on the one key number (log 11-08).
6. Payoff last, end right after it; last line loops back to the hook word so replays feel natural.
7. Earn the send, don't demand it: make one line so useful that it names who needs it and hands them something usable: a date, a number, a check ("If you know someone on OPT, this is the date they need"). Instagram said it won't recommend content it believes explicitly asks for shares, comments or tags to "hack" reach; it called open-ended questions and CTAs fine. Use one open question ("what would you do?") over "comment YES" or a keyword. Like-asks stay rare, and only as the owner's call (max 2 engagement lines per Reel, playbook 00d).
8. Score each Reel on Mosseri's top three signals: average watch time, sends per reach, likes per reach. Sends matter slightly more for reaching non-followers, so judge growth Reels on sends per reach, not likes.
9. Write for sound-off scrollers too, and keep text readable: frame-1 text must state the stake on its own in at most 5 words (a 7-word line takes about 3 s to read), live captions in 2-3-word groups, and B-roll cards show only the key word or number, never a second sentence next to the captions. Keep captions on; spell visa and tech terms right.
10. Length is only a penalty when people leave: Instagram counts seconds watched and share watched. Default 25-45 s; our 79 s Reel lost ~70% at the start. Who it affects and what changed must both be said by second 10 (about 30 spoken words); go longer only with 3+ real reveals.
11. Frame 1 = the owner's face + the stake, from 0.0 s: no fade-in, logo or title card (feed decisions take ~1.7 s; faces register in ~0.1 s). Our no-face/text-first openings had our worst skips (86-93%), but a face alone isn't enough (CA/CFA 75% skip): pair it with a clear stake. Look into the lens for hook and payoff; at the key number, look or point toward it on screen so the eye follows (log 11-10).
12. Problem-led stories use PAIN → POKE → FIX → PAYOFF → RECAP (owner's reference, 11-10): line 1 = a pain/situation the viewer has already lived ("You missed Nvidia..."); 1-2 lines on why the OBVIOUS move fails ("...but buying at the open is how most people overpay"); a short step-by-step fix (max 3 steps, one line each); show proof it works (a number, a real case); last line names the first-line pain again as solved. For pure news, keep HOOK→DELAY→REHOOK→PAYOFF but make the payoff a FIX the viewer can use.

### 2026-11-06: On-screen text, sound-off viewing and reading speed
Source quality: survey numbers are self-reported and partly vendor-commissioned; the reading-speed and redundancy findings are peer-reviewed or textbook research on subtitles and instructional video, not on Reels, so they transfer by analogy. Netflix's subtitle style guide could not be opened in this run, so no Netflix figure is used.

**Findings**
1. Many people scroll with the sound off. Verizon Media + Publicis Media (online survey, 5,616 US adults aged 18-54, April 2019): 69% watch video with sound off in public, 25% even in private; 80% of people who use captions are not hearing impaired. [Streaming Media](https://www.streamingmedia.com/Articles/News/Online-Video-News/80-of-Video-Caption-Users-Arent-Hearing-Impaired-Finds-Verizon-131860.aspx). Coverage of the same study also reports that 80% say they are more likely to watch a whole video when captions are available (self-report, not measured completion). [Next TV](https://nexttv.com/news/mobile-videos-often-watched-without-audio-study-finds). Counterpoint: TikTok's own Nielsen study (2020, platform-commissioned) says 88% of TikTok users call sound "vital to the TikTok experience." [TikTok for Business](https://ads.tiktok.com/business/en-US/blog/evolution-of-sound-volume-1)
   - Apply: write every Reel for both kinds of viewer. The sound-off scroller decides from the frame-1 text alone, so that text must carry the stake on its own and must not only make sense once you hear the voice ("This rule could cut your 60 days", not "Wait for it..."). Sound-on viewers still get the voice, music and tone change.
   - Frame-1 text examples (fill [ ] with verified facts only): "H-1B layoff? [N] days, not 60" / "OpenAI just [verified action]" / "Your OPT clock: [verified change]".
2. When text moves too fast, people stop reading it fully and understand less. Kruger, Wisniewska & Liao (Applied Psycholinguistics, 2021) tested subtitles at 12, 20 and 28 characters per second: comprehension fell as speed rose, and faster subtitles were read less completely, with fewer words reread. [Cambridge Core](https://core-prod.cambridgecore.org/core/journals/applied-psycholinguistics/article/why-subtitle-speed-matters-evidence-from-word-skipping-and-rereading/A7DB0CBF5910353143738CF9C1721317)
   - Our own math (not from the paper): a 7-word hook is about 35-45 characters, so it takes about 3 s to read at 12-15 cps. That is longer than our 2-second hook window. Harry and the owner talk at about 3 words/s (about 16-18 cps), so live captions are already near the fast end.
   - Apply: frame-1 hook text should be at most 5 words (about 25 characters), or stay on screen for at least 3 s. Keep live captions in 2-3-word groups. In the fastest stretch (numbers, lists), slow the voice slightly or put the number alone on screen.
3. Repeating every spoken word as on-screen text on top of a busy graphic can hurt understanding. Clark & Mayer, e-Learning and the Science of Instruction, ch. 7, the redundancy principle: "In general, do not add printed text to a narrated graphic." Its boundary conditions say the extra text can help when there is no graphic, when the pace is slow, when the narration uses technical or unfamiliar words, or when the text is shorter than the narration (key words). [O'Reilly](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/e-learning-and-the/9781119158660/c07.xhtml)
   - Limits: this is learning research in a classroom-style setting. Sound-off viewers on Instagram need captions anyway, so this does NOT mean dropping captions.
   - Apply: during B-roll, let the card show only the key word or number ("60 → 0 DAYS", "$6.3B"), not a second full sentence next to the captions. Our audience hears technical terms (OPT, STEM extension, I-140) and many are non-native English speakers, which is the boundary case where captions help, so keep captions on and spell those terms correctly.
   - Re-commit/open-loop lines that read well as captions (short, one idea each): "That's the small change. The big one is next." / "Now look at this number." / "Here's who it hits first."

**Our metrics on this** (notes/metrics.md): Binance (11-03) opened on a busy split screen with "Okay, this is..." and had 76% skip. Midterms (11-04) had one clear audience in the hook and 59% skip. This fits "one idea and readable text in frame 1", but the topic and the opening line also changed, so it is not proof.

**Test next:** for the next 3 Reels, cap the frame-1 text at 5 words, show only the key word or number on B-roll cards (no sentence cards), and keep captions in 2-3-word groups. Compare skip rate against the recent median of about 60% (Midterms 59.8%, NY 60.3%, Ambani 58.6%) and average watch time against about 16 s. Log the frame-1 text and its word count in each metrics row so we can compare.

### 2026-11-07: Curiosity gaps and open loops: what makes them work, and when "clickbait" backfires
Source quality: Kang et al. is a peer-reviewed lab study with small samples (19 fMRI, 16 pupil/memory) using trivia, not video. Molina et al. is peer-reviewed (CHI 2021) but about news headlines with MTurk users plus Facebook share data. The Engaging News Project and KQED results are summarised by journalism outlets; KQED gave no numbers. None of this is Reels data, so it transfers by analogy.

**Findings**
1. Curiosity peaks when people are about 50/50 on the answer, and they will pay or wait to get it. Kang, Hsu, Krajbich, Loewenstein, McClure, Wang & Camerer, "The Wick in the Candle of Learning" (Psychological Science, 2009): "Reported curiosity was indeed an inverted-U-shaped function of P, reaching its maximum when P was around .50." People spent scarce tokens or waited longer for answers they were more curious about. Pupils widened in the 1-2 s before the answer appeared, more so for high-curiosity questions. Answers to high-curiosity questions they had guessed wrong were remembered better 11-16 days later. This builds on Loewenstein's information-gap theory (1994): curiosity is the feeling of a gap between what you know and what you want to know. [Kang et al. 2009, PDF (CMU)](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/WickCandleLearning.pdf)
   - Apply: open loops work best on things our viewer half-knows: the H-1B grace period, OPT days, a FAANG salary, an OpenAI valuation. A gap about something they know nothing about is too wide (no guess, no itch); one they already know is closed. A "guess the number" beat makes them commit to a guess, and a wrong guess makes the fact stick (good for saves and for repeating it at work, which is our North Star).
   - Keep the pause short: the anticipation in the study built over about 1-2 s. On screen: the question as 3-5 words, a 1-2 s beat (a ticking sound or blur), then the number pops.
   - Lines (fill [ ] with verified facts only): "Quick guess: how many days does a laid-off H-1B worker get to find a new job? ... [verified number]." / "Guess what OpenAI is valued at now. Higher. Higher. [verified figure]." / "Most people guess [common belief]. It's actually [verified fact]."
2. Vague clickbait does not beat a plain, specific line, and question-style hooks do worst. Molina, Sundar, Rony, Hassan, Le & Lee, "Does Clickbait Actually Attract More Clicks?" (CHI 2021): "clickbait did not generate more curiosity than non-clickbait"; in a controlled test of one story, "no statistically significant differences between clickbait and non-clickbait headlines in terms of likelihood of clicking and sharing." In the first study, plain headlines got more clicks than question, modal ("could", "might") and superlative headlines; in real Facebook share data, lists, "wh" words and "this/these" headlines did better than plain ones (but the four clickbait detectors agreed only 47% of the time). [Molina et al. 2021, PDF (Penn State)](https://pike.psu.edu/publications/chi21.pdf)
3. Question headlines cost credibility. Engaging News Project (Scacco & Muddiman, 2,057 US adults, 2016): question headlines drew more negative reactions, lower expectations and less willingness to engage than traditional or forward-reference ones; forward-reference ("This is the...") and traditional headlines tested the same. "Individuals in some ways expect the news to answer questions for them, not necessarily pose questions." [Journalism.co.uk](https://www.journalism.co.uk/readers-perceive-question-based-headlines-more-negatively-study-shows/). KQED's science-headline experiment found reading and sharing intent did not differ by format, but question headlines were seen as less credible ("millennials in particular") and less likely to be called real news. [KQED](https://www.kqed.org/about/13722/experimenting-with-science-news-headline-format-to-maximize-engagement)
   - Apply (findings 2-3): open with a specific statement that carries its own gap, not a question ("Is your H-1B safe?") and not "could/might". "This"/"here's the" and numbered lists are fine. Our audience reads us as a news source, so credibility is the asset; a news page that opens with a vague question looks like bait. The open question belongs at the END (toolkit 7), where it asks for an opinion, not in the hook.
   - This softens the brief's "Is Elon trolling us, or is this real?" hook type: use it only when the second half is a real verified answer, and say the claim first.
   - Hook rewrites: "Is H-1B getting harder?" → "This H-1B rule cuts your [verified] days." / "Could AI take your job?" → "[Company] just cut [verified number] jobs. Here's the team it hit." / "What is OpenAI worth?" → "OpenAI is now worth more than [verified comparison]."

**Our metrics on this** (notes/metrics.md): our best hold (xAI, 42% skip, 21.7 s) opened with a bold specific claim and promised a twist at the end, which fits finding 2. Hook wording is not logged per Reel, so we cannot yet check question vs statement hooks in our own data. Log the exact spoken first line in each metrics row.

**Test next:** for the next 3 Reels, use a statement hook (no question in the first line) and one "guess the number" beat at about 40-60% of the runtime, on a fact the viewer half-knows. Check the retention chart for a drop or flat line at the guess beat, compare avg watch time against about 16 s and saves per reach against the recent median (about 0.35%, 11-06 snapshot: Ambani 0.34%, Binance 0.52%, Midterms 0.25%), and skip rate against about 60%. A single Reel is not a result.

### 2026-11-08: Voice pacing, energy and Reel length
Source quality: Guo, Kim & Rubin is a large but retrospective log study of edX lecture videos (correlation, not a controlled test). Guyer et al. is a peer-reviewed set of six experiments on persuasive audio messages (N = 3,958), not social video. The Instagram statements are second-hand (trade press and creator blogs paraphrasing Mosseri or quoting an event slide), and they partly disagree with each other. Everything transfers to Reels by analogy.

**Findings**
1. Length was the strongest predictor of how much people watched, and faster, more energetic speakers held people longer. Guo, Kim & Rubin, "How Video Production Affects Student Engagement" (ACM Learning at Scale 2014, 6.9 million viewing sessions): "Video length was by far the most significant indicator of engagement"; "median engagement time is at most 6 minutes, regardless of total video length." "Students generally engaged more with videos where instructors spoke faster": within a length range, engagement rose by up to about 2x from the slowest (48-130 wpm) to the fastest speakers (range up to 254 wpm, mean 156). The authors warn that "speaking rate is merely a surface feature that correlates with enthusiasm," so their advice is to coach enthusiasm, not to force speed, and to tell speakers "they do not need to purposely slow down." Talking-head segments also held people better than slides alone. [Guo et al. 2014, PDF (MIT)](https://up.csail.mit.edu/other-pubs/las2014-pguo-engagement.pdf)
   - Apply: energy comes first. The owner should sound like he is telling a friend something surprising, not reading. Cutting to his face at the turn ("here's the part nobody's talking about") is supported here too.
   - Re-commit lines with built-in energy (fill [ ] with verified facts only): "Okay, but that's not the crazy part." / "Now here's where it hits you." / "And this is the number that made me stop scrolling: [verified number]."
2. Faster speech helps only up to a point, then it hurts. Guyer, Vaughan-Johnston, Fabrigar et al., "Vocal Speed and Processing of Persuasive Messages: Curvilinear Processing Effects" (Journal of Nonverbal Behavior, 2025, six studies, N = 3,958): "faster speed initially increases perceived source confidence and message processing," but the effect levels off and reverses at very fast speeds, where listeners could no longer tell strong arguments from weak ones. In Experiment 1, careful processing was strongest at moderate rates (about 148-192 wpm). The authors make no claim about a "perfect" speed. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10919-024-00477-6)
   - Our math (not from the paper): the owner and Harry talk at about 3 words/s, which is about 180 wpm, already inside that range. So do not speed up further in editing (no 1.2x voice, no jump-cutting every breath), especially on the line that carries the key fact. Speed up the setup; slow down and pause briefly (about 1 s) on the one number or rule change you want them to remember and repeat at work.
   - Pacing pattern for a 35 s Reel: fast hook (claim) → fast context → slow, clear key fact with the number alone on screen → fast "why it matters for you" → slow last line that loops to the hook.
3. Instagram has given mixed signals on length, and watch time counts both seconds and share watched. Social Media Today (May 2024) reported an Instagram creator-event slide saying "Reels that are longer than 90 seconds can hurt distribution." [Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-longer-reels-hurt-performance). In 2025 Mosseri was paraphrased as saying Instagram weighs both the percentage of a Reel watched and the total seconds watched, and that "the app is not punishing creators that post longer content" (paraphrase, Feb 2025). [Digital Information World](https://www.digitalinformationworld.com/2025/02/instagram-provides-more-insights-on.html). A later Q&A quote attributed to him: "Shoot Reels of any length, but make sure they have a compelling, attention-grabbing start that hooks viewers for at least 10 seconds." (May 2025, unverified secondary source) [Quasa](https://quasa.io/media/instagram-head-adam-mosseri-addresses-video-view-algorithm-it-s-all-about-the-first-10-seconds)
   - Read: length itself is not the penalty; losing people is. A long Reel only works if every extra second is held. Our own data says we are not holding long Reels yet: Canva (79 s) sat at about 70% skip and 10 s average watch, while the 30-45 s Reels did better (metrics 11-06 lesson). So the playbook's 25-40 s default stands; go longer only for a story with 3+ real reveals.
   - Apply "the first 10 seconds": the hook and the re-commit beat must both land before 10 s. A good check: if the script's 10-second mark (about 30 spoken words) has not yet said who this affects and what changed, cut the setup.

**Our metrics on this** (notes/metrics.md): spoken pace and runtime are not logged per Reel, so we cannot yet check pace against watch time. The length pattern above (79 s Canva vs shorter Reels) also changed topic and freshness at the same time, so it is directional only.

**Test next:** for the next 3 Reels, keep runtime at 45 s or less, count script words ÷ runtime and keep it at about 165-190 wpm, and use one deliberate slow-down + 1 s pause on the key number. Log runtime, words per minute and the second where the key number lands in each metrics row. Compare average watch time as a percentage of runtime (xAI was about 21.7 s; work out its % once runtime is logged) and average watch time against about 16 s, plus saves per reach against about 0.35% (the slowed-down number is the thing to save). Check the retention chart: there should be no drop right after the slow beat. A single Reel is not a result.

### 2026-11-09: Why people share (send) vs why they click: emotion and arousal
Source quality: Berger & Milkman is a peer-reviewed field study (New York Times most-emailed list) plus small lab experiments (N = 45-49 each). Robertson et al. is a large set of randomized headline tests on Upworthy, but the data is from 2013-2015 and measures clicks, not shares. The NYT "Psychology of Sharing" numbers are an industry study (survey of 2,500 heavy sharers plus focus groups), read here only through a 2012 secondary summary. None of this is Reels data, so it transfers by analogy.

**Findings**
1. High-arousal emotions get shared; sadness does not. Berger & Milkman, "What Makes Online Content Viral?" (Journal of Marketing Research, 2012; ~7,000 NYT articles, Aug-Nov 2008): positive content was more viral than negative, but awe, anger and anxiety (all high-arousal) all raised the chance of making the most-emailed list, while sadness (low-arousal) lowered it. A one-standard-deviation rise in anger raised the odds by 34%, in awe by 30%. Practical utility, interest and surprise also predicted sharing. In the lab, more amusement or anger raised sharing and more sadness lowered it, and arousal explained the effect: "Arousal mediated the impact of emotion on social transmission." [Berger & Milkman, PDF (Wharton)](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/11/Virality.pdf)
   - Apply: before scripting, name the ONE emotion the Reel should leave: awe ("wait, that's possible?"), justified anger (a company did something unfair, stated as fact), anxiety WITH an action ("here's what to check"), or amusement. Do not leave people sad. A layoff story told as "people lost their jobs" is sad and low-energy; told as "here's the clock that starts for H-1B workers the day they're laid off, and what to do in week one" it becomes anxiety plus utility, which is what gets sent.
   - Lines (fill [ ] with verified facts only): "This is the part that should make you angry: [verified fact]." / "Wait, an AI can now [verified capability]? Here's the proof." / "If you're on H-1B, this is the one date to write down: [verified date]."
2. Negative words stop people, but only a little, and sad works for clicks, not fear. Robertson, Pröllochs, Schwarzenegger, Pärnamets, Van Bavel & Feuerriegel, "Negativity drives online news consumption" (Nature Human Behaviour, 2023; 22,743 randomized headline tests, ~105,000 variants, 370 million impressions): "For a headline of average length, each additional negative word increased the click-through rate by 2.3%"; each positive word lowered it by about 1.0%. Sadness raised clicks slightly; joy and fear lowered them; anger had no significant effect. The authors call the effect small and note that clicking is private while sharing is "public, curated and influenced by a myriad of social factors." [Robertson et al. 2023, PDF (LMU)](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/94951/1/s41562-023-01538-4.pdf); [Nieman Lab summary](https://www.niemanlab.org/2023/03/negative-words-in-news-headlines-generate-more-clicks-but-sad-words-are-more-effective-than-angry-or-scary-ones/)
   - Apply (findings 1-2 together): what stops the scroll and what gets sent are not the same thing. A negative stake in the hook ("you lose", "cut", "banned") can help the stop a little; scare words alone ("terrifying", "nightmare") did not. Then turn the energy up for the body and end on something the viewer can DO or say, so it becomes send-worthy.
   - Hook lines (true-only): "This rule cuts [verified number] days from your OPT clock." / "[Company] just lost [verified figure]. Here's who pays for it."
3. People send things that are useful to the person they send them to, and that say something about themselves. NYT "Psychology of Sharing" (industry study, survey of 2,500 medium-to-heavy sharers): 94% said they carefully consider how useful the information will be to the recipient; 84% share to support causes, 78% to grow relationships, 69% for self-fulfillment, 68% to define themselves to others. [Contently summary, 2012](https://contently.com/strategist/2012/02/24/psychology-of-sharing)
   - Apply: the recipient is the real audience of a send. Give the sender a "gift" to pass on: one date, one number, one check, one tool, said in a single sentence they could paste into a group chat. This sharpens toolkit 2 and 7: "who needs this" works best when the line hands that person something usable.

### 2026-11-10: The first second: what the eye grabs before the brain decides (faces, gaze, front-loading)
Source quality: the Facebook IQ numbers are Meta's own 2016-2017 ad research, read through trade press (Social Media Today, Marketing Dive); Meta's original post could not be opened in this run, and sample details for the recall study are not public. Crouzet & Thorpe is peer-reviewed lab vision science (still images, not video). Sajjacholapunt & Ball is a peer-reviewed eye-tracking study of 72 students looking at banner ads on fake webpages. None of this is Reels data, so it transfers by analogy; our own metrics (below) are the stronger evidence here.

**Findings**
1. The scroll decision is made in well under 2 seconds, and people take in something from a quarter of a second. Facebook IQ (2016): people spend "on average, 1.7 seconds with a piece of content on mobile compared to 2.5 seconds on desktop," and "it now takes only 0.25 seconds of exposure for people to recall mobile feed content" at a statistically significant rate. In its review of 850+ video ads watched sound-off for the first 10 s, "only 23% of the ads had a clear brand message within that first segment"; its advice was to put the key message up front in a form that does not depend on audio. [Social Media Today, Apr 2016](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/facebook-releases-new-guide-to-help-advertisers-maximize-video-ad-performan/450818/). Facebook also said people scroll "41% faster on a smartphone than on a desktop" and cited Nielsen: impressions under 2 s still drove 38% of ad recall. [Marketing Dive, Jun 2017](https://www.marketingdive.com/news/facebook-why-mobile-video-ads-must-work-fast/446217/)
   - Apply: frame 1 is not a warm-up. No fade-in from black, no logo sting, no title card, no "Okay so" (already a rule in the brief). The very first frame must already show the stake: face + the 3-5-word hook text + (if it exists) the number. Check it by pausing the export at 0.0 s and 0.3 s: if a stranger can't tell what the Reel is about from that still, rework it.
   - Frame-1 text examples (fill [ ] with verified facts only): "OPT rule: [verified change]" / "[Company] cut [verified N] jobs" / "H-1B: [verified N] days".
2. Faces win the race for the eye. Crouzet & Thorpe (Frontiers in Psychology, 2011), building on Crouzet, Kirchner & Thorpe (2010): people can launch "extremely rapid saccades toward faces in natural scenes that can be initiated only 100 ms after image onset," and when asked to look at vehicles instead, faces pulled the eye so strongly that "performance is particularly poor, especially when the saccades are initiated at short latencies." In their replication, eye movements to faces were faster and more accurate (168 ms, 88.8% correct) than to vehicles (197 ms, 72.8%). [Crouzet & Thorpe 2011, PDF (Frontiers)](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2011.00342/pdf)
   - Apply: a human face is the fastest thing to register in the 0.25-1.7 s window, so the owner's face should be in frame 1 of every Reel (not B-roll, not a text card). Use a big, well-lit face (fills roughly the top half), with the hook text placed so it does not cover the eyes.
3. Where the face LOOKS steers where the viewer looks, and what they remember. Sajjacholapunt & Ball, "The influence of banner advertisements on attention and memory: human faces with averted gaze can enhance advertising effectiveness" (Frontiers in Psychology, 2014; N = 72, eye-tracking): a face looking toward the ad's text and product "increased attention to the banner overall, as well as to the advertising text and product," while with a face looking straight at the viewer "the focus of attention was localized more on the face region rather than on the text or product." Brand recognition scores were 1.50 (gaze toward the ad), 1.06 (direct gaze) and 0.54 (no face); implicit memory 64.5% vs 47.8% vs 41.5%. Limits: fake brands, static banners, students, exploratory interactions. [Sajjacholapunt & Ball 2014, PDF (Frontiers)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00166/pdf)
   - Apply: use the two kinds of gaze on purpose. Direct-to-camera for the hook and the payoff (it holds attention on the person, which is what stops the scroll and builds trust). At the one key number or rule, the owner turns his eyes or points toward where the number pops on screen (split screen, or number placed at the side he looks to), so the viewer's eye goes to the fact we want them to remember and send. This fits the 11-08 "[slow] beat on the key number."
   - Lines that pair with a look or point toward the number (fill [ ] with verified facts only): "Look at this number. [verified number]." / "This is the date that matters: [verified date]." / "Right here, this is the part that changes for you."

**Our metrics on this** (notes/metrics.md, strongest evidence today): no-face openings lost every time. Modal (10-17, no face) 3.0 s avg watch; Meta Muse (10-21, text wall, no face until 4 s) 93% skip, our worst; text-only Reel (10-26) 86% skip. Owner-face hooks carried every top Reel (xAI 42% skip, Midterms 59%, AGI, Hanuman, WhatsApp 54%). Counter-example: CA/CFA (11-04) opened on the owner's face and still had 74.7% skip and 6.3 s avg, so a face is necessary but not enough: the face must come with a clear stake in frame 1. Gaze direction at the key number has never been tried or logged.

**Test next:** for the next 3 owner Reels, (a) pause-check frame 1 at 0.0 s (face + stake text visible, no fade/logo), and (b) at the key-number beat, have the owner look or point toward the on-screen number placed beside him. Log "frame-1 = face+text? y/n" and "gaze cue at number? y/n" in each metrics row. Compare skip rate against the ~60% median (a) and saves per reach against ~0.35% plus the retention chart at the number beat (b). A single Reel is not a result.
