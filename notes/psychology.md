# Scroll psychology & retention notes (living file)
Read by every script run (playbook §00d). A daily scheduled research task appends new dated findings at the bottom.
Keep the "CURRENT TOOLKIT" section short (max 15 bullets) and up to date; log raw findings in the dated log.

## CURRENT TOOLKIT (use in every script)
1. Autopilot breaker: the first line must contradict a belief the viewer holds, or reveal a stake for THEM ("Everyone on H-1B thinks they get 60 days... A new rule wants to make it zero."). Say it as a statement, not a question: question headlines tested worse on credibility and engagement and vague clickbait did not beat plain headlines (log 11-07).
2. Social currency and emotion: give one fact their friends don't know yet, or a ready-made opinion. Name the one emotion the Reel leaves: awe, justified anger, anxiety WITH an action, or amusement. Never end sad: in research sadness lowers sharing (our Canva layoffs Reel: 3 saves, 0 shares, though length also differed). Negative stake words help the stop only a little; the send comes from a usable line (log 11-09). Triggers: tie the story to ONE verified dated or recurring cue the viewer will hit again ("Thursday after the bell", "starts [date]") and say it again at the end; cued things keep getting talked about, merely interesting ones fade. Breaking news goes stale within hours: post same day (log 11-19). Follows come from familiarity + trust, not one great Reel: run 2-3 named recurring series (same day, same frame-1 wording, series name in the first 2 s), new story each time; post on a rhythm the owner can sustain (gap days cost growth); test off-format ideas as Trial Reels (log 11-22).
3. Re-commit beat within 3-6 s of the hook: a second reason to stay ("Watch till the end, number 3 is the one nobody's talking about" / "Save this, you'll need it on Nov 10").
4. Open loops every 8-10 s: numbered items ("rule two... rule three is the quiet one"), "and that's not even the worst part", a question you answer later. Make each gap specific and about something the viewer half-knows (visa days, salaries, a famous company), where a guess feels 50/50; that is where curiosity peaks. A "guess before I say it" beat with a short pause, then the answer, is the strongest form. Close every loop you open.
5. Pattern interrupt every 2-3 s on screen, but only RELATED cuts: punch-in, new angle, number pop, or B-roll that shows exactly what the voice is saying (the person, the filing, the chart circled). Unrelated/generic stock cuts cost attention and lower memory (log 11-12). In high-stakes stories hold ONE clean ~2 s shot on the key fact (fast cuts + arousing content = overload). Add a sound cue (whoosh, music drop, short silence) plus a voice tone change at the re-commit and rehook. If a music bed is used: instrumental only (no lyrics or second voice under his line), not drum-heavy, ~15 LU under the voice, and [music out] on the key number; a bed under narration cut recall by about a third in tests, and many viewers hear English as a second language (log 11-23). Voice: energy over speed. Keep the natural fast pace (about 3 words/s, 165-190 wpm), never speed the voice up in editing, and slow down with a ~1 s pause only on the one key number (log 11-08).
6. End on a peak, then cut, and loop: people remember a clip by its strongest moment and its last moment, not its length (peak-end rule), and the send/save decision happens there. Last line = the most usable fact or the smart line, said with energy; no recap, outro card, fade or "follow for more". Write the last line so it runs straight into the first spoken line, same framing as frame 1, so an auto-replay sounds continuous (Instagram counts replays as views). No "part 2" cliffhanger without part 1's payoff: unfinished stories aren't remembered better; tease the next Reel in one line and link it (log 11-13).
7. Earn the send, don't demand it: make one line so useful that it names who needs it and hands them something usable: a date, a number, a check ("If you know someone on OPT, this is the date they need"). Instagram said it won't recommend content it believes explicitly asks for shares, comments or tags to "hack" reach; it called open-ended questions and CTAs fine. Use one open question ("what would you do?") over "comment YES" or a keyword. Like-asks stay rare, and only as the owner's call (max 2 engagement lines per Reel, playbook 00d). Saves: usefulness predicted sharing about as strongly as awe (NYT data), and people save what they can't remember but trust will be there; so when a Reel has 3+ verified dates, steps or tickers, put them on ONE "save card" frame (≤3 lines, held ~2 s) and give a reason to come back ("check back Thursday"). No save ask on one-fact or opinion Reels. Saves are a supporting signal, not a lever over sends (log 11-20).
8. Score each Reel on Mosseri's top three signals: average watch time, sends per reach, likes per reach. Sends matter slightly more for reaching non-followers, so judge growth Reels on sends per reach, not likes. Then read the retention chart on day 2: note the steepest drop after 3 s (and the line spoken there) and the like-timing peak. Next script: a re-commit line 1-2 s before that dip type (usually the hook-to-background switch), and the like-peak type of line moved inside the first 10 s. A re-watch bump on a number card can mean it flashed too fast (log 11-16).
9. Write for sound-off scrollers too, and keep text readable: frame-1 text must state the stake on its own in at most 5 words (a 7-word line takes about 3 s to read), live captions in 2-3-word groups, and B-roll cards show only the key word or number, never a second sentence next to the captions. Keep captions on; spell visa and tech terms right. Big numbers: about half of adults space million/billion/trillion evenly, so never compare across them unconverted; say the key number exactly as the source does (precise sounds more confident, never invent precision), then ONE perspective line (rank, ratio or unit change, calculator-checked), which improved recall of news numbers in tests (log 11-17).
10. Length is only a penalty when people leave: Instagram counts seconds watched and share watched. Default 25-45 s, but it is a soft default: our 79 s Canva Reel lost ~70% at the start, while the 72 s GTA Reel (3 face windows, a reveal every ~15 s) held 24.9 s, our best real hold (log 11-22). Who it affects and what changed must both be said by second 10 (about 30 spoken words); go past 45 s only with 3+ real reveals.
11. Frame 1 = the owner's face + the stake, from 0.0 s: no fade-in, logo or title card (feed decisions take ~1.7 s; faces register in ~0.1 s). Our no-face/text-first openings had our worst skips (86-93%), but a face alone isn't enough (CA/CFA 75% skip): pair it with a clear stake. Look into the lens for hook and payoff; at the key number, look or point toward it on screen so the eye follows (log 11-10).
12. Problem-led stories use PAIN → POKE → FIX → PAYOFF → RECAP (owner's reference, 11-10): line 1 = a pain/situation the viewer has already lived ("You missed Nvidia..."); 1-2 lines on why the OBVIOUS move fails ("...but buying at the open is how most people overpay"); a short step-by-step fix (max 3 steps, one line each); show proof it works (a number, a real case); last line names the first-line pain again as solved. For pure news, keep HOOK→DELAY→REHOOK→PAYOFF but make the payoff a FIX the viewer can use. When the source names a real person, use ONE (not a group) with their photo/clip as a 2-5 s doorway, then widen to "you" and give the real base rate; say so if the case is extreme. Never invent or composite a person. Optional boost, not a rule: our best day-1 Reel (Week stocks) had no single person (log 11-18).
13. Identity call-out: put ONE big-audience identity word the viewer is in (H-1B, OPT, laid off, Indian tech workers) first in line 1 and in the frame-1 text, as a statement with a stake ("H-1B: your clock just changed"), never a "which one are you?" label list (our CA/CFA "CA, CFA or LLB?" hook: 75% skip, 0 shares vs Midterms' F-1/OPT/H-1B stake: 60% skip, 10 shares). Keep "you/your" on the key fact (self-related facts are remembered better), pair it with something they can DO, and switch to "someone you know" only in the send line. For boosted Reels avoid "Are you [attribute]?" copy (Meta ad policy on personal attributes) (log 11-11).
14. Comments come from what the story leaves open, not from asking: name the consequence for a group and a date when it gets decided, and end on one question about a REAL uncertainty or two-sided disagreement (never a comment keyword). Our first comment = a full sentence with one extra verified fact + a question (top-of-thread comments get the most replies; very short ones get almost none). Reply in the owner's voice: praise the specific point, ask one follow-up, skip trolls (log 11-14). Unproven for reach; log comments per reach.
15. Make it unmistakably ours, and show receipts: Instagram stops recommending accounts whose posts are mostly others' content; credit, watermarks, crops or speed changes don't count, voice-over/commentary does. Every Reel = owner's face + voice + a take or "what it means for you"; news clips only as evidence under his voice, never reposted with just text. At the key fact, flash the source for ~1 s on the same shot ("Source: SEC filing") and add a "Sources:" line in the caption: in a 2,000-person test, a sources label raised trust in short news videos (log 11-15) Calibrate: say facts flat and confident, label takes and predictions "my theory... because" (a confident source caught wrong is trusted least), hedge with the source's range not filler words, and correct real errors ourselves in a pinned comment (log 11-21). Credibility is the #1 reason people trust a creator, then "like me": one TRUE similarity or track-record line per Reel max ("I said X on [date]; here's how it's going"), never a claimed status he doesn't have (log 11-22).

### 2026-11-05: Instagram's own ranking guidance and what it says about asks
Source quality: Mosseri and Instagram quotes below are as reported by Social Media Today, not read on Instagram directly. Instagram's own ranking explainer could not be opened in this run, and Meta's Transparency Center pages returned no body text.

**Findings**
1. Mosseri (Jan 2025): "The top three signals that matter most for ranking are watch time, likes and sends." He pointed creators to average watch time, likes per reach and sends per reach. "Likes are slightly more important for connected content, and sends are slightly more important for unconnected content." [Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-shares-algorithm-insights-2025/738034/)
   - Apply: for growth, the story needs a clear person who needs it. Saves are not in that top three, so playbook 00's "saves second" is our own priority, not Mosseri's stated ranking.
   - Lines (fill [ ] with verified facts only): "If you're on OPT, the number that matters here is [verified number]." / "Know someone on H-1B? This is the date they need: [verified date]."
2. Instagram (2024): "If we believe your content explicitly asks for engagement through shares, comments, tags, or other actions, then we won't recommend it." Its example was asking people to comment a specific word, number or emoji. [Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-says-using-certain-ctas-can-impact-post-reach/717732/)
3. Instagram then clarified: "There haven't been changes to our recommendation guidelines." The test it gave is intent: it is engagement bait "if the end goal is to simply maximize the quantity of engagements as a way to 'hack' reach." "Open-ended questions and calls-to-action are a great way to engage with your audience." [Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-clarifies-advice-single-word-ctas-longer-reels/718151/)
   - Limits: the original video was later deleted, the statements are from 2024, and nothing public shows how a spoken ask is detected. Treat this as a risk to manage, not a proven penalty.
   - Apply: this weakens the old toolkit claim that save/send asks are safe. Our "send this to..." triggers, "YES/NO" comment asks and "like this video" lines are all explicit asks, so they carry some of this risk. "Watch till the end" asks for no share, comment or tag, and it was in our best-held Reel (xAI).
   - Lines: "Would you take that deal? Tell me why." / "Who actually wins here? I want to hear your reasoning." / "I think [labelled take]. Change my mind."
4. Instagram's retention chart shows the percentage of views still watching at each moment; skip rate is reported as the share leaving in the first three seconds (secondary definition, not an Instagram quote). [Website Builder Expert](https://www.websitebuilderexpert.com/news/instagram-reel-retention-chart/)
   - Apply: use the chart to place open loops. Before scripting, note where the last Reel's curve dropped, then put the next reveal just ahead of that point.
   - Re-commit lines: "But that's the small number. The one that affects you is next." / "Here's where it gets strange."

### 2026-11-06: On-screen text, sound-off viewing and reading speed
Source quality: survey numbers are self-reported and partly vendor-commissioned; the reading-speed and redundancy findings are peer-reviewed or textbook research on subtitles and instructional video, not on Reels, so they transfer by analogy. Netflix's subtitle style guide could not be opened in this run, so no Netflix figure is used.

**Findings**
1. Many people scroll with the sound off. Verizon Media + Publicis Media (online survey, 5,616 US adults aged 18-54, April 2019): 69% watch video with sound off in public, 25% even in private; 80% of people who use captions are not hearing impaired. [Streaming Media](https://www.streamingmedia.com/Articles/News/Online-Video-News/80-of-Video-Caption-Users-Arent-Hearing-Impaired-Finds-Verizon-131860.aspx). Coverage of the same study also reports that 80% say they are more likely to watch a whole video when captions are available (self-report, not measured completion). [Next TV](https://nexttv.com/news/mobile-videos-often-watched-without-audio-study-finds). Counterpoint: TikTok's own Nielsen study (2020, platform-commissioned) says 88% of TikTok users call sound "vital to the TikTok experience." [TikTok for Business](https://ads.tiktok.com/business/en-US/blog/evolution-of-sound-volume-1)
   - Apply: write every Reel for both kinds of viewer. The sound-off scroller decides from the frame-1 text alone, so that text must carry the stake on its own and must not only make sense once you hear the voice ("This rule could cut your 60 days", not "Wait for it..."). Sound-on viewers still get the voice, music and tone change.
   - Frame-1 text examples (fill [ ] with verified facts only): "H-1B layoff? [N] days, not 60" / "OpenAI just [verified action]" / "Your OPT clock: [verified change]".
2. When text moves too fast, people stop reading it fully and understand less. Kruger, Wisniewska & Liao (Applied Psycholinguistics, 2021) tested subtitles at 12, 20 and 28 characters per second: comprehension fell as speed rose, and faster subtitles were read less completely, with fewer words reread. [Cambridge Core](https://core-prod.cambridgecore.org/core/journals/applied-psycholinguistics/article/why-subtitle-speed-matters-evidence-from-word-skipping-and-rereading/A7DB0CBF5910353143738CF9C1721317)
   - Our own math (not from the paper): a 7-word hook is about 35-45 characters, so it takes about 3 s to read at 12-15 cps. That is longer than our 2-second hook window. Harry and the owner talk at about 3 words/s (about 16-18 cps), so live captions are already near the fast end.
   - Apply: frame-1 hook text should be at most 5 words (about 25 characters), or stay on screen for at least 3 s. Keep live captions in 2-3-word groups. In the fastest stretch (numbers, lists), slow the voice slightly or put the number alone on screen.
3. Repeating every spoken word as on-screen text on top of a busy graphic can hurt understanding. Clark & Mayer, e-Learning and the Science of Instruction, ch. 7, the redundancy principle: "In general, do not add printed text to a narrated graphic." Its boundary conditions say the extra text can help when there is no graphic, when the pace is slow, when the narration uses technical or unfamiliar words, or when the text is shorter than the narration (key words). [O'Reilly](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/e-learning-and-the/9781119158660/c07.xhtml)
   - Limits: this is learning research in a classroom-style setting. Sound-off viewers on Instagram need captions anyway, so this does NOT mean dropping captions.
   - Apply: during B-roll, let the card show only the key word or number ("60 → 0 DAYS", "$6.3B"), not a second full sentence next to the captions. Our audience hears technical terms (OPT, STEM extension, I-140) and many are non-native English speakers, which is the boundary case where captions help, so keep captions on and spell those terms correctly.
   - Re-commit/open-loop lines that read well as captions (short, one idea each): "That's the small change. The big one is next." / "Now look at this number." / "Here's who it hits first."

**Our metrics on this** (notes/metrics.md): Binance (11-03) opened on a busy split screen with "Okay, this is..." and had 76% skip. Midterms (11-04) had one clear audience in the hook and 59% skip. This fits "one idea and readable text in frame 1", but the topic and the opening line also changed, so it is not proof.

**Test next:** for the next 3 Reels, cap the frame-1 text at 5 words, show only the key word or number on B-roll cards (no sentence cards), and keep captions in 2-3-word groups. Compare skip rate against the recent median of about 60% (Midterms 59.8%, NY 60.3%, Ambani 58.6%) and average watch time against about 16 s. Log the frame-1 text and its word count in each metrics row so we can compare.

### 2026-11-07: Curiosity gaps and open loops: what makes them work, and when "clickbait" backfires
Source quality: Kang et al. is a peer-reviewed lab study with small samples (19 fMRI, 16 pupil/memory) using trivia, not video. Molina et al. is peer-reviewed (CHI 2021) but about news headlines with MTurk users plus Facebook share data. The Engaging News Project and KQED results are summarised by journalism outlets; KQED gave no numbers. None of this is Reels data, so it transfers by analogy.

**Findings**
1. Curiosity peaks when people are about 50/50 on the answer, and they will pay or wait to get it. Kang, Hsu, Krajbich, Loewenstein, McClure, Wang & Camerer, "The Wick in the Candle of Learning" (Psychological Science, 2009): "Reported curiosity was indeed an inverted-U-shaped function of P, reaching its maximum when P was around .50." People spent scarce tokens or waited longer for answers they were more curious about. Pupils widened in the 1-2 s before the answer appeared, more so for high-curiosity questions. Answers to high-curiosity questions they had guessed wrong were remembered better 11-16 days later. This builds on Loewenstein's information-gap theory (1994): curiosity is the feeling of a gap between what you know and what you want to know. [Kang et al. 2009, PDF (CMU)](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/WickCandleLearning.pdf)
   - Apply: open loops work best on things our viewer half-knows: the H-1B grace period, OPT days, a FAANG salary, an OpenAI valuation. A gap about something they know nothing about is too wide (no guess, no itch); one they already know is closed. A "guess the number" beat makes them commit to a guess, and a wrong guess makes the fact stick (good for saves and for repeating it at work, which is our North Star).
   - Keep the pause short: the anticipation in the study built over about 1-2 s. On screen: the question as 3-5 words, a 1-2 s beat (a ticking sound or blur), then the number pops.
   - Lines (fill [ ] with verified facts only): "Quick guess: how many days does a laid-off H-1B worker get to find a new job? ... [verified number]." / "Guess what OpenAI is valued at now. Higher. Higher. [verified figure]." / "Most people guess [common belief]. It's actually [verified fact]."
2. Vague clickbait does not beat a plain, specific line, and question-style hooks do worst. Molina, Sundar, Rony, Hassan, Le & Lee, "Does Clickbait Actually Attract More Clicks?" (CHI 2021): "clickbait did not generate more curiosity than non-clickbait"; in a controlled test of one story, "no statistically significant differences between clickbait and non-clickbait headlines in terms of likelihood of clicking and sharing." In the first study, plain headlines got more clicks than question, modal ("could", "might") and superlative headlines; in real Facebook share data, lists, "wh" words and "this/these" headlines did better than plain ones (but the four clickbait detectors agreed only 47% of the time). [Molina et al. 2021, PDF (Penn State)](https://pike.psu.edu/publications/chi21.pdf)
3. Question headlines cost credibility. Engaging News Project (Scacco & Muddiman, 2,057 US adults, 2016): question headlines drew more negative reactions, lower expectations and less willingness to engage than traditional or forward-reference ones; forward-reference ("This is the...") and traditional headlines tested the same. "Individuals in some ways expect the news to answer questions for them, not necessarily pose questions." [Journalism.co.uk](https://www.journalism.co.uk/readers-perceive-question-based-headlines-more-negatively-study-shows/). KQED's science-headline experiment found reading and sharing intent did not differ by format, but question headlines were seen as less credible ("millennials in particular") and less likely to be called real news. [KQED](https://www.kqed.org/about/13722/experimenting-with-science-news-headline-format-to-maximize-engagement)
   - Apply (findings 2-3): open with a specific statement that carries its own gap, not a question ("Is your H-1B safe?") and not "could/might". "This"/"here's the" and numbered lists are fine. Our audience reads us as a news source, so credibility is the asset; a news page that opens with a vague question looks like bait. The open question belongs at the END (toolkit 7), where it asks for an opinion, not in the hook.
   - This softens the brief's "Is Elon trolling us, or is this real?" hook type: use it only when the second half is a real verified answer, and say the claim first.
   - Hook rewrites: "Is H-1B getting harder?" → "This H-1B rule cuts your [verified] days." / "Could AI take your job?" → "[Company] just cut [verified number] jobs. Here's the team it hit." / "What is OpenAI worth?" → "OpenAI is now worth more than [verified comparison]."

**Our metrics on this** (notes/metrics.md): our best hold (xAI, 42% skip, 21.7 s) opened with a bold specific claim and promised a twist at the end, which fits finding 2. Hook wording is not logged per Reel, so we cannot yet check question vs statement hooks in our own data. Log the exact spoken first line in each metrics row.

**Test next:** for the next 3 Reels, use a statement hook (no question in the first line) and one "guess the number" beat at about 40-60% of the runtime, on a fact the viewer half-knows. Check the retention chart for a drop or flat line at the guess beat, compare avg watch time against about 16 s and saves per reach against the recent median (about 0.35%, 11-06 snapshot: Ambani 0.34%, Binance 0.52%, Midterms 0.25%), and skip rate against about 60%. A single Reel is not a result.

### 2026-11-08: Voice pacing, energy and Reel length
Source quality: Guo, Kim & Rubin is a large but retrospective log study of edX lecture videos (correlation, not a controlled test). Guyer et al. is a peer-reviewed set of six experiments on persuasive audio messages (N = 3,958), not social video. The Instagram statements are second-hand (trade press and creator blogs paraphrasing Mosseri or quoting an event slide), and they partly disagree with each other. Everything transfers to Reels by analogy.

**Findings**
1. Length was the strongest predictor of how much people watched, and faster, more energetic speakers held people longer. Guo, Kim & Rubin, "How Video Production Affects Student Engagement" (ACM Learning at Scale 2014, 6.9 million viewing sessions): "Video length was by far the most significant indicator of engagement"; "median engagement time is at most 6 minutes, regardless of total video length." "Students generally engaged more with videos where instructors spoke faster": within a length range, engagement rose by up to about 2x from the slowest (48-130 wpm) to the fastest speakers (range up to 254 wpm, mean 156). The authors warn that "speaking rate is merely a surface feature that correlates with enthusiasm," so their advice is to coach enthusiasm, not to force speed, and to tell speakers "they do not need to purposely slow down." Talking-head segments also held people better than slides alone. [Guo et al. 2014, PDF (MIT)](https://up.csail.mit.edu/other-pubs/las2014-pguo-engagement.pdf)
   - Apply: energy comes first. The owner should sound like he is telling a friend something surprising, not reading. Cutting to his face at the turn ("here's the part nobody's talking about") is supported here too.
   - Re-commit lines with built-in energy (fill [ ] with verified facts only): "Okay, but that's not the crazy part." / "Now here's where it hits you." / "And this is the number that made me stop scrolling: [verified number]."
2. Faster speech helps only up to a point, then it hurts. Guyer, Vaughan-Johnston, Fabrigar et al., "Vocal Speed and Processing of Persuasive Messages: Curvilinear Processing Effects" (Journal of Nonverbal Behavior, 2025, six studies, N = 3,958): "faster speed initially increases perceived source confidence and message processing," but the effect levels off and reverses at very fast speeds, where listeners could no longer tell strong arguments from weak ones. In Experiment 1, careful processing was strongest at moderate rates (about 148-192 wpm). The authors make no claim about a "perfect" speed. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10919-024-00477-6)
   - Our math (not from the paper): the owner and Harry talk at about 3 words/s, which is about 180 wpm, already inside that range. So do not speed up further in editing (no 1.2x voice, no jump-cutting every breath), especially on the line that carries the key fact. Speed up the setup; slow down and pause briefly (about 1 s) on the one number or rule change you want them to remember and repeat at work.
   - Pacing pattern for a 35 s Reel: fast hook (claim) → fast context → slow, clear key fact with the number alone on screen → fast "why it matters for you" → slow last line that loops to the hook.
3. Instagram has given mixed signals on length, and watch time counts both seconds and share watched. Social Media Today (May 2024) reported an Instagram creator-event slide saying "Reels that are longer than 90 seconds can hurt distribution." [Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-longer-reels-hurt-performance). In 2025 Mosseri was paraphrased as saying Instagram weighs both the percentage of a Reel watched and the total seconds watched, and that "the app is not punishing creators that post longer content" (paraphrase, Feb 2025). [Digital Information World](https://www.digitalinformationworld.com/2025/02/instagram-provides-more-insights-on.html). A later Q&A quote attributed to him: "Shoot Reels of any length, but make sure they have a compelling, attention-grabbing start that hooks viewers for at least 10 seconds." (May 2025, unverified secondary source) [Quasa](https://quasa.io/media/instagram-head-adam-mosseri-addresses-video-view-algorithm-it-s-all-about-the-first-10-seconds)
   - Read: length itself is not the penalty; losing people is. A long Reel only works if every extra second is held. Our own data says we are not holding long Reels yet: Canva (79 s) sat at about 70% skip and 10 s average watch, while the 30-45 s Reels did better (metrics 11-06 lesson). So the playbook's 25-40 s default stands; go longer only for a story with 3+ real reveals.
   - Apply "the first 10 seconds": the hook and the re-commit beat must both land before 10 s. A good check: if the script's 10-second mark (about 30 spoken words) has not yet said who this affects and what changed, cut the setup.

### 2026-11-09: Why people share (send) vs why they click: emotion and arousal
Source quality: Berger & Milkman is a peer-reviewed field study (New York Times most-emailed list) plus small lab experiments (N = 45-49 each). Robertson et al. is a large set of randomized headline tests on Upworthy, but the data is from 2013-2015 and measures clicks, not shares. The NYT "Psychology of Sharing" numbers are an industry study (survey of 2,500 heavy sharers plus focus groups), read here only through a 2012 secondary summary. None of this is Reels data, so it transfers by analogy.

**Findings**
1. High-arousal emotions get shared; sadness does not. Berger & Milkman, "What Makes Online Content Viral?" (Journal of Marketing Research, 2012; ~7,000 NYT articles, Aug-Nov 2008): positive content was more viral than negative, but awe, anger and anxiety (all high-arousal) all raised the chance of making the most-emailed list, while sadness (low-arousal) lowered it. A one-standard-deviation rise in anger raised the odds by 34%, in awe by 30%. Practical utility, interest and surprise also predicted sharing. In the lab, more amusement or anger raised sharing and more sadness lowered it, and arousal explained the effect: "Arousal mediated the impact of emotion on social transmission." [Berger & Milkman, PDF (Wharton)](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/11/Virality.pdf)
   - Apply: before scripting, name the ONE emotion the Reel should leave: awe ("wait, that's possible?"), justified anger (a company did something unfair, stated as fact), anxiety WITH an action ("here's what to check"), or amusement. Do not leave people sad. A layoff story told as "people lost their jobs" is sad and low-energy; told as "here's the clock that starts for H-1B workers the day they're laid off, and what to do in week one" it becomes anxiety plus utility, which is what gets sent.
   - Lines (fill [ ] with verified facts only): "This is the part that should make you angry: [verified fact]." / "Wait, an AI can now [verified capability]? Here's the proof." / "If you're on H-1B, this is the one date to write down: [verified date]."
2. Negative words stop people, but only a little, and sad works for clicks, not fear. Robertson, Pröllochs, Schwarzenegger, Pärnamets, Van Bavel & Feuerriegel, "Negativity drives online news consumption" (Nature Human Behaviour, 2023; 22,743 randomized headline tests, ~105,000 variants, 370 million impressions): "For a headline of average length, each additional negative word increased the click-through rate by 2.3%"; each positive word lowered it by about 1.0%. Sadness raised clicks slightly; joy and fear lowered them; anger had no significant effect. The authors call the effect small and note that clicking is private while sharing is "public, curated and influenced by a myriad of social factors." [Robertson et al. 2023, PDF (LMU)](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/94951/1/s41562-023-01538-4.pdf); [Nieman Lab summary](https://www.niemanlab.org/2023/03/negative-words-in-news-headlines-generate-more-clicks-but-sad-words-are-more-effective-than-angry-or-scary-ones/)
   - Apply (findings 1-2 together): what stops the scroll and what gets sent are not the same thing. A negative stake in the hook ("you lose", "cut", "banned") can help the stop a little; scare words alone ("terrifying", "nightmare") did not. Then turn the energy up for the body and end on something the viewer can DO or say, so it becomes send-worthy.
   - Hook lines (true-only): "This rule cuts [verified number] days from your OPT clock." / "[Company] just lost [verified figure]. Here's who pays for it."
3. People send things that are useful to the person they send them to, and that say something about themselves. NYT "Psychology of Sharing" (industry study, survey of 2,500 medium-to-heavy sharers): 94% said they carefully consider how useful the information will be to the recipient; 84% share to support causes, 78% to grow relationships, 69% for self-fulfillment, 68% to define themselves to others. [Contently summary, 2012](https://contently.com/strategist/2012/02/24/psychology-of-sharing)
   - Apply: the recipient is the real audience of a send. Give the sender a "gift" to pass on: one date, one number, one check, one tool, said in a single sentence they could paste into a group chat. This sharpens toolkit 2 and 7: "who needs this" works best when the line hands that person something usable.
   - Lines: "Send-proof version: [verified date], [verified rule], done." (as on-screen card, not a spoken demand) / "If your roommate's on OPT, this is the sentence they need: [verified fact]." / "Say this at work: [labelled one-line take]."

**Our metrics on this** (notes/metrics.md 11-06): Canva layoffs (11-05), a sad-type story, got 3 saves but 0 shares and 70% skip; Midterms (11-03), a high-stakes "you" story posted on the day (anxiety + action), got 10 shares, our most. This fits finding 1, but length (79 s vs shorter), freshness and topic also differed, so it is directional only.

**Test next:** for the next 3 Reels, write the target emotion (awe / anger / anxiety+action / amusement) at the top of each script and log it in the metrics row. No Reel ends on a sad note; layoff or cut stories must end on a concrete "what to do / what to check" line. Compare sends per reach against the ~0.42% median (11-05 table) and log shares on every row. A single Reel is not a result.

### 2026-11-10: The first second: what the eye grabs before the brain decides (faces, gaze, front-loading)
Source quality: the Facebook IQ numbers are Meta's own 2016-2017 ad research, read through trade press (Social Media Today, Marketing Dive); Meta's original post could not be opened in this run, and sample details for the recall study are not public. Crouzet & Thorpe is peer-reviewed lab vision science (still images, not video). Sajjacholapunt & Ball is a peer-reviewed eye-tracking study of 72 students looking at banner ads on fake webpages. None of this is Reels data, so it transfers by analogy; our own metrics (below) are the stronger evidence here.

**Findings**
1. The scroll decision is made in well under 2 seconds, and people take in something from a quarter of a second. Facebook IQ (2016): people spend "on average, 1.7 seconds with a piece of content on mobile compared to 2.5 seconds on desktop," and "it now takes only 0.25 seconds of exposure for people to recall mobile feed content" at a statistically significant rate. In its review of 850+ video ads watched sound-off for the first 10 s, "only 23% of the ads had a clear brand message within that first segment"; its advice was to put the key message up front in a form that does not depend on audio. [Social Media Today, Apr 2016](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/facebook-releases-new-guide-to-help-advertisers-maximize-video-ad-performan/450818/). Facebook also said people scroll "41% faster on a smartphone than on a desktop" and cited Nielsen: impressions under 2 s still drove 38% of ad recall. [Marketing Dive, Jun 2017](https://www.marketingdive.com/news/facebook-why-mobile-video-ads-must-work-fast/446217/)
   - Apply: frame 1 is not a warm-up. No fade-in from black, no logo sting, no title card, no "Okay so" (already a rule in the brief). The very first frame must already show the stake: face + the 3-5-word hook text + (if it exists) the number. Check it by pausing the export at 0.0 s and 0.3 s: if a stranger can't tell what the Reel is about from that still, rework it.
   - Frame-1 text examples (fill [ ] with verified facts only): "OPT rule: [verified change]" / "[Company] cut [verified N] jobs" / "H-1B: [verified N] days".
2. Faces win the race for the eye. Crouzet & Thorpe (Frontiers in Psychology, 2011), building on Crouzet, Kirchner & Thorpe (2010): people can launch "extremely rapid saccades toward faces in natural scenes that can be initiated only 100 ms after image onset," and when asked to look at vehicles instead, faces pulled the eye so strongly that "performance is particularly poor, especially when the saccades are initiated at short latencies." In their replication, eye movements to faces were faster and more accurate (168 ms, 88.8% correct) than to vehicles (197 ms, 72.8%). [Crouzet & Thorpe 2011, PDF (Frontiers)](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2011.00342/pdf)
   - Apply: a human face is the fastest thing to register in the 0.25-1.7 s window, so the owner's face should be in frame 1 of every Reel (not B-roll, not a text card). Use a big, well-lit face (fills roughly the top half), with the hook text placed so it does not cover the eyes.
3. Where the face LOOKS steers where the viewer looks, and what they remember. Sajjacholapunt & Ball, "The influence of banner advertisements on attention and memory: human faces with averted gaze can enhance advertising effectiveness" (Frontiers in Psychology, 2014; N = 72, eye-tracking): a face looking toward the ad's text and product "increased attention to the banner overall, as well as to the advertising text and product," while with a face looking straight at the viewer "the focus of attention was localized more on the face region rather than on the text or product." Brand recognition scores were 1.50 (gaze toward the ad), 1.06 (direct gaze) and 0.54 (no face); implicit memory 64.5% vs 47.8% vs 41.5%. Limits: fake brands, static banners, students, exploratory interactions. [Sajjacholapunt & Ball 2014, PDF (Frontiers)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00166/pdf)
   - Apply: use the two kinds of gaze on purpose. Direct-to-camera for the hook and the payoff (it holds attention on the person, which is what stops the scroll and builds trust). At the one key number or rule, the owner turns his eyes or points toward where the number pops on screen (split screen, or number placed at the side he looks to), so the viewer's eye goes to the fact we want them to remember and send. This fits the 11-08 "[slow] beat on the key number."
   - Lines that pair with a look or point toward the number (fill [ ] with verified facts only): "Look at this number. [verified number]." / "This is the date that matters: [verified date]." / "Right here, this is the part that changes for you."

**Our metrics on this** (notes/metrics.md, strongest evidence today): no-face openings lost every time. Modal (10-17, no face) 3.0 s avg watch; Meta Muse (10-21, text wall, no face until 4 s) 93% skip, our worst; text-only Reel (10-26) 86% skip. Owner-face hooks carried every top Reel (xAI 42% skip, Midterms 59%, AGI, Hanuman, WhatsApp 54%). Counter-example: CA/CFA (11-04) opened on the owner's face and still had 74.7% skip and 6.3 s avg, so a face is necessary but not enough: the face must come with a clear stake in frame 1. Gaze direction at the key number has never been tried or logged.

**Test next:** for the next 3 owner Reels, (a) pause-check frame 1 at 0.0 s (face + stake text visible, no fade/logo), and (b) at the key-number beat, have the owner look or point toward the on-screen number placed beside him. Log "frame-1 = face+text? y/n" and "gaze cue at number? y/n" in each metrics row. Compare skip rate against the ~60% median (a) and saves per reach against ~0.35% plus the retention chart at the number beat (b). A single Reel is not a result.

- 2026-11-10 (owner reference, Hindi creator): 'Pain, Poke, Fix, Payoff, Recap' script framework. Pain = relatable situation in line 1 ("that happens to me too"), Poke = why the obvious answer doesn't work, Fix = clear step-by-step, Payoff = visible proof, Recap = return to the line-1 pain and show it's solved (gives satisfaction). ### 2026-11-11: Identity call-outs ("If you're on H-1B..."): why "you" grabs attention, and when naming a group backfires
Source quality: Symons & Johnson is a peer-reviewed meta-analysis of lab memory studies (word lists, not video). The own-name finding is a classic lab listening task (Moray 1959; Wood & Cowan 1995; Conway, Cowan & Bunting 2001), not social media. Noar et al. is a peer-reviewed meta-analysis of printed health messages. The Meta ad-policy detail is from a secondary summary (Jon Loomer), because Meta's policy page returned no body text in this run. Our own metrics (below) are the most direct evidence today.

**Findings**
1. Information tied to "me" is remembered better than the same information processed for meaning, and better than information tied to someone else. Symons & Johnson, "The Self-Reference Effect in Memory: A Meta-Analysis" (Psychological Bulletin, 1997, 129 effect sizes): self-referent encoding beat semantic encoding (mean d = 0.65) and beat other-referent encoding by a smaller margin (d = 0.35). The authors explain it as "the self is a well-developed and often-used construct" that helps people elaborate and organise new facts. [Symons & Johnson 1997, PDF (CORE)](https://core.ac.uk/download/194456453.pdf)
   - Apply: the key fact must be framed as something happening to the viewer's own clock, salary or job, not as abstract news ("USCIS changed a rule" → "Your 60-day clock just changed"). That is what makes it stick and get repeated at work (North Star). "You" beats "workers" even when the fact is identical.
   - The gap between self (d 0.65) and other (d 0.35) also suggests a split: the HOOK is about "you"; the SEND line (toolkit 7) switches to "someone you know", because by then the viewer already holds the fact and the job is to hand it on.
   - Lines (fill [ ] with verified facts only): "If you're on OPT, your clock now [verified change]." / "This changes your next paycheck if you work at [verified company]." / later in the same Reel: "Know someone on H-1B? This is the one date they need: [verified date]."
2. A personal cue can break through even when people are not paying attention, but only for some of them. In dichotic-listening studies, "approximately 33% of subjects report hearing their own name in an unattended, irrelevant message" (Conway, Cowan & Bunting, Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 2001, replicating Moray 1959 and Wood & Cowan 1995); those who noticed it had lower working-memory capacity, i.e. were more easily distracted. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758%2FBF03196169)
   - Our reading (analogy, not Reels data): autopilot scrolling is "unattended" attention. A word that is part of the viewer's own identity ("H-1B", "OPT", "laid off", "your 401k") works like hearing your name across a noisy room: it reaches some people who were not listening. It does not reach everyone, so the call-out must sit next to a real stake, not replace it.
   - Put the identity word FIRST in the spoken line and on the frame-1 text (≤5 words): "H-1B: [verified N] days" beats "New rule could affect some visa holders".
3. Tailoring helps, but only a little on average, and scare-tailoring can hurt. Noar, Benac & Harris, "Does Tailoring Matter?" (Psychological Bulletin, 2007; 57 studies, 58,454 people): the weighted mean effect of tailored vs generic messages on behaviour was small, r = .074, and depended on eight moderators, including what the message was tailored on. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17592961/). A later summary of that review (Michie et al.) reports that tailoring on attitudes and self-efficacy went with larger effects, while tailoring on perceived susceptibility ("this could happen to you") went with smaller ones. [Michie et al., PDF (CORE)](https://core.ac.uk/download/17050054.pdf)
   - Apply: the call-out gets attention; what keeps people is the "what you can do" part. "If you're on H-1B, you're in danger" is susceptibility-only. "If you're on H-1B, here's the one check you can do this week" is identity + self-efficacy. This matches toolkit 2 ("anxiety WITH an action") and the PAIN → FIX format (toolkit 12).
4. Platform risk for paid promotion: Meta's ad standards say ads must not "assert or imply the personal attributes" of the audience; "you/your" is fine unless it names a listed attribute. The list as summarised includes national origin, financial status, health and criminal record; immigration status is not named in that summary, so whether "If you're on H-1B" passes ad review is unclear. [Jon Loomer](https://www.jonloomer.com/can-we-use-the-words-you-and-your-in-facebook-ads-copy/); policy page: [Meta Transparency Center](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/objectionable-content/privacy-violations-personal-attributes/)
   - Apply: this is about ads only, not organic Reels. If a Reel will be boosted, prefer "For anyone on H-1B..." / "H-1B workers: ..." over "Are you on H-1B?" or "Your visa..." in the boosted caption and frame-1 text. Never use an attribute in a negative "you" way ("Struggling with debt?"). National origin is on the list, so "If you're Indian..." in boosted copy is also a risk; "Indian tech workers: ..." is safer.

**Our metrics on this** (notes/metrics.md): two identity hooks, opposite results. Midterms (11-03/04) named a BIG group the viewer is in (F-1/OPT/H-1B, posted on election day) with a clear stake: 59.7% skip, 17.7 s avg, 10 shares (our record). CA/CFA (11-04) opened on a label list ("CA, CFA or LLB?") as a question: 74.7% skip, 6.3 s avg, 0 shares; the 11-04 metrics note already suspected it "filters too hard in the first second." Read: a call-out works when (a) it names a group most of our audience is in, (b) it is a statement with a stake, not a "which one are you?" question, and (c) only one identity, not three. Topic, timing and length also differed, so this is directional only.

**Test next:** for the next 3 owner Reels, open with ONE big-audience identity word + the stake as a statement in the first spoken line and the frame-1 text ("H-1B: [verified change]"), keep "you/your" on the key fact, and switch to "someone you know" only in the send line. Log the identity word used (or "none") in each metrics row. Compare skip rate against ~60% and sends per reach against the ~0.42% median (11-05 table). A single Reel is not a result.

### 2026-11-12: Pattern interrupts and cut frequency: when a cut helps attention and when it costs memory
Source quality: the Lang studies are peer-reviewed lab experiments on TV messages (small student samples, physiological and reaction-time measures); I read abstracts or database summaries, not full papers, so exact cut rates and sample sizes are not given here. Geiger & Reeves full text was read. Anderson's attentional-inertia work is mostly on children watching TV (read via his encyclopedia entry). A 2026 TikTok brand-video study (Paekivi & Karjus, arXiv) found editing pace associated with engagement but did not say which pace level won and its editing labels had low reliability, so it is not used. None of this is Reels data; it transfers by analogy.

**Findings**
1. Cuts inside the same scene raise attention and memory without overloading people. Lang, Zhou, Schwartz, Bolls & Potter, "The effects of edits on arousal, attention, and memory for television messages: When an edit is an edit can an edit be too much?" (Journal of Broadcasting & Electronic Media, 2000): rates of edits (camera changes within the same scene) went "from slow to very fast"; "as the rate of edits increases physiological arousal, self-reported arousal, and memory increase," and edits can raise attention and encoding "without significantly increasing cognitive load." [CityU Scholars](https://scholars.cityu.edu.hk/en/publications/the-effects-of-edits-on-arousal-attention-and-memory-for-televisi/)
   - Apply: our "pattern interrupt every 2-3 s" rule is supported when the new frame stays on the same story: punch-in on the owner's face, zoom on the same document, a second angle of the same person, the number popping onto the same shot. These are the cheap, safe interrupts.
2. A cut to something unrelated costs attention and lowers memory for what comes next. Lang, Geiger, Strickwerda & Sumner, "The Effects of Related and Unrelated Cuts on Television Viewers' Attention, Processing Capacity, and Memory" (Communication Research, 1993): unrelated cuts slowed reaction times (they used more processing capacity) and viewers remembered information after related cuts better than after unrelated cuts, more so for visual memory. [Scilit](https://scilit.com/publications/9e903f69b2eb53c627dfcd43c49c48f2). Geiger & Reeves, "We interrupt this program... Attention for television sequences" (Human Communication Research, 1993; N = 26): "The greatest differences between attention to the related and unrelated cuts were found 1 second after their onset," and an unrelated interrupt needed more attention when people had already watched the first segment for 30 s rather than 10 s. [Geiger & Reeves 1993, PDF](https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Geiger-HumanCommRes93.pdf)
   - Apply: every B-roll cut must SHOW what the voice is saying at that moment (the CEO named, the filing, the logo, the chart with the number circled). Generic stock B-roll (random servers, typing hands, "AI brain" loops) is an unrelated cut: it spends the viewer's attention for about a second and hurts memory of the line said over it. Never put the key number over an unrelated cut. Our Nvidia/Jensen Reel (11-10: 46% skip, 20.7 s avg, 6 shares, our best day 1) used real news B-roll with the key person circled, which fits this.
   - Script cue: write a [SHOW: ...] note next to each line so the editor cuts to the matching thing ("Jensen sold [verified] shares [SHOW: the filing, number circled]").
3. Fast cutting plus high-stakes content overloads people and they remember less. Lang et al., "The effects of production pacing and arousing content on the information processing of television messages" (Journal of Broadcasting & Electronic Media, 1999): fast pace and arousing content each raised arousal and "the allocation of processing resources to messages," but together they overloaded processing and reduced recognition and cued recall of message content. [ACNP record](https://acnpsearch.tweb-dev.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/67463345). A Missouri PRIME Lab student study of political ads (Roehrick & Bolls, 2005, 12 ads; university news release, not peer-reviewed) found the most memorable ads had fewer camera angles and scene changes, and Bolls pointed to candidates "look directly at the camera while talking." [Missouri School of Journalism](https://journalism.missouri.edu/2005/12/research-reveals-slower-paced-non-attack-political-ads-are-most-attention-getting)
   - Apply: our stories are often high-arousal already (visa clocks, layoffs, billion-dollar fights). In those, the fast cutting belongs in setup and context; at the ONE key fact, hold a single clean shot for about 2 s (face to camera, or the number alone on screen). This matches the 11-08 [slow] beat and the 11-10 gaze cue: slow voice + still frame + one number = what they remember and send.
   - Lines for the held beat (fill [ ] with verified facts only): "Stop. This is the number. [verified number]." / "Read that again: [verified date]." / "That's it. That's the whole rule: [verified rule]."
4. Sound changes pull eyes back, and attention that has lasted keeps going. Daniel R. Anderson (Encyclopedia of Communication and Information, on children's TV research): a change of speaker, sound effects or music onset "consistently attract looking" when a viewer is not watching, and cuts and movement are the features most consistently tied to looking; with "attentional inertia," the longer a look lasts, the less likely the viewer is to look away in the next second. [Encyclopedia.com](https://encyclopedia.com/media/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/childrens-attention-television)
   - Our reading (children's TV, analogy only): the half-watching scroller is caught back by an AUDIO change, not only a visual one. Put a sound cue (whoosh, music drop or switch, a beat of silence) at the re-commit line and the rehook. Inertia also means the first 5-10 s are the expensive part: once someone has watched that long, they tend to keep going, so spend most cut variety early and keep the middle steady but changing.
   - Re-commit lines to pair with a sound cue: "[music drops] Okay, now the part that affects you." / "[whoosh] And here's who actually pays for it." / "[silence, 0.5 s] Nobody's talking about this bit."

**Our metrics on this** (notes/metrics.md): cut type is not logged. The Nvidia/Jensen Reel (related news B-roll, person circled, face only at hook/rehook/end) is our best day-1 Reel (46.1% skip, 20.7 s avg); older Reels with generic AI B-roll sat at 60-70% skip, but topic, hook and length also changed, so this is directional only.

**Test next:** for the next 3 Reels, (a) every B-roll shot must match the spoken line (no generic stock AI loops; mark [SHOW: ...] in the script), (b) hold one clean ~2 s shot on the key number, and (c) add a sound cue at the re-commit and rehook. Log "generic B-roll used? y/n" and "held shot on key number? y/n" in each metrics row. Compare average watch time against ~16 s (and Nvidia's 20.7 s), saves per reach against ~0.35%, and check the retention chart for a dip right after any B-roll switch. A single Reel is not a result.

### 2026-11-13: Endings: the last seconds, replays and loops, and "part 2" cliffhangers
Source quality: Fredrickson & Kahneman is a peer-reviewed lab study using short film clips (small student samples, 1993); Alaybek et al. is a peer-reviewed meta-analysis (174 effect sizes) of the peak-end rule across many kinds of experience, not social video. Ghibellini & Meier is a peer-reviewed 2025 meta-analysis of interrupted-task studies (lab tasks, not media). The Instagram and YouTube view-counting changes are platform announcements read through trade press (Social Media Today; PPC Land quoting a TeamYouTube Help Community post). The Mosseri watch-time point is a creator blogger's paraphrase of his video, not a quote. None of this is Reels data, so it transfers by analogy.

**Findings**
1. People judge a clip by its strongest moment and its ending, not by its length. Fredrickson & Kahneman, "Duration neglect in retrospective evaluations of affective episodes" (Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1993; 32 + 96 students rating pleasant and aversive film clips in short and long versions): global ratings were "reasonably well predicted by an unweighted combination of the most extreme affect experienced during the episode and by affect at the end moments," and evaluations worked "as if duration did not matter" (for pleasant clips, length had no reliable effect). [Fredrickson & Kahneman 1993, PDF (UF)](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/fredr-kahneman-jpsp1993.pdf). A meta-analysis found the peak-end effect "large" (r = 0.581, 95% CI 0.487-0.661) and the effect of duration "essentially nil." Alaybek et al., Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 2022. [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v170y2022ics0749597822000334.html)
   - Our reading: the send, save and follow decisions happen at the END of a Reel, right when the viewer's memory of it is being formed. So the last 2-3 s are worth as much as the hook. A Reel that peaks at 70% and then trails off with a recap, a logo, "follow for more" or a flat summary leaves a weaker memory than one that ends on its best line.
   - Apply: end ON the peak or right after it. The last spoken line should be the most usable or most surprising sentence (the smart line, the key date, the verdict), said with energy, then cut. No outro card, no fade, no "that's it for today". Keep the playbook's "best reveal at ~70%" but make the closing line a second, smaller peak, not a wind-down. This also backs "never end sad" (toolkit 2).
   - Closing lines (fill [ ] with verified facts only): "So the one date to remember: [verified date]. Everything else is noise." / "That's why [company] isn't scared. [Rival] is." (only if both halves are verified or labelled as a take) / "Say this at work: [labelled one-line take]."
2. Replays count, and so do seconds watched. Instagram (Aug 2024): "repeat views of content will be counted, similar to how plays and replays are counted for reels." [Social Media Today](https://socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-updates-metrics-to-focus-creators-on-views/723645/). YouTube Shorts from 31 Mar 2025 counts a view "from the moment a video starts to play or replay," keeping the old metric as "engaged views." [PPC Land](https://ppc.land/youtube-changes-how-shorts-views-are-counted-from-march-31/). Mosseri (paraphrased, Feb 2025) said Instagram weighs both the percentage watched and total seconds watched. [Lindsey Gamble](https://www.lindseygamble.com/blog/instagram-reveals-how-its-algorithm-weighs-watch-time-for-reels)
   - Limits: no platform says how much a replay is worth in ranking; the "views" change is about counting, not proof that loops are boosted. Treat a loop ending as cheap upside, not a hack.
   - Apply: a seamless loop means the last words run straight into the first spoken line, so an auto-replay sounds like one sentence and the viewer stays for a second pass (more seconds, share watched above 100%). Build it at script stage: write the hook first, then write the last line so it ends on the word that leads into it. Visually, end on the same framing as frame 1 (owner's face, same crop), no end card.
   - Loop pairs (hook / last line; fill [ ] with verified facts only): Hook "H-1B: your clock just changed." / End "...which is exactly why, if you're on H-1B," → loops into "your clock just changed." / Hook "[Company] just lost [verified figure]." / End "and nobody at [company] saw it coming, because" → loops into the hook. Keep the end line true on its own; never cut a fact mid-sentence.
3. Unfinished things don't stick in memory, but people do want to finish them. Ghibellini & Meier, "Interruption, recall and resumption: a meta-analysis of the Zeigarnik and Ovsiankina effects" (Humanities and Social Sciences Communications, 2025): "We found no memory advantage for unfinished tasks but found a general tendency to resume tasks"; the memory advantage "has proven particularly difficult to replicate." [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/palcom/v12y2025i1d10.1057_s41599-025-05000-w.html)
   - Apply: open loops work INSIDE the Reel because people want to resume what was started (supports toolkit 4: open, then close). But a "part 2 tomorrow" cliffhanger should not count on viewers remembering the gap the next day; the Zeigarnik "they'll keep thinking about it" claim does not hold up. If we do a series, close the main loop in part 1, tease the next one in a single line, and link part 2 explicitly (pinned comment, caption, "part 1" in the frame-1 text of part 2). Never end part 1 without its payoff: that ends on frustration, which hurts the end-memory from finding 1.
   - Series tease that still closes the loop: "That's the rule. What it does to your green card timeline is tomorrow's Reel." (only if that Reel is actually planned)

Views ÷ reach is a rough replay/repeat-view proxy: Anthropic IPO 2,033/1,569 = 1.30 (day 1), Nvidia/Jensen 1,914/1,535 = 1.25 (day 2), Canva 650/357 = 1.82, CA/CFA 433/278 = 1.56. The older Reels are higher, so this ratio also grows with age and repeat exposure; it cannot separate replays yet. Anthropic IPO, which ended on a practical "before you invest" fix, got our record 21 shares and 8 saves, which fits "end on the usable peak", but hook, topic and timing also differed, so this is directional only.

### 2026-11-14: Comments: what makes people reply (without engagement bait)
Source quality: all three are peer-reviewed or university studies of news comment sections (German newspapers and a US TV station's Facebook page), not Reels. Weber and Ziegele et al. are correlational content analyses; I read Weber's abstract via Crossref, Ziegele et al.'s abstract via a university repository, and the per-factor numbers for both only through an American Press Institute summary (its table values are not defined there, so treat them as relative likelihoods, not exact odds). The Stroud et al. field experiment is read through Journalism.co.uk. Comment counts are not in our metrics table, so our own data cannot check this yet.

**Findings**
1. Some stories pull comments and some don't, and it is about what the story is, not how the comment box works. Weber, "Discussions in the comments section" (New Media & Society, 2014; content analysis of political news, adapting Galtung & Ruge's news values): news factors of an article predicted both how many people commented and how interactive the thread became. [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177%2F1461444813495165). As summarised by the American Press Institute (1,000 articles, three German newspapers): "Articles that describe why they matter to specific groups of people generate more comments"; stories about consequences or analysis drew more comments, events with a clear endpoint drew more than ongoing situations, and international and purely fact-focused stories drew fewer. [American Press Institute](https://americanpressinstitute.org/?p=3496)
   - Apply: a Reel that only reports "X happened" gives people nothing to say. Comments come from the consequence for a named group and from a decision with an end point ("the rule takes effect on [date]", "the vote is [date]"). Our audience is US-based but many stories are international to our Indian viewers, which is another reason to bring every story back to "what it does to you".
   - Lines (fill [ ] with verified facts only): "So here's what this actually does to anyone on OPT: [verified consequence]." / "This gets decided on [verified date]. After that, it's done."
2. Comments that carry uncertainty, controversy, negativity or a personal angle get replies; very short ones don't. Ziegele, Breiner & Quiring, "What Creates Interactivity in Online News Discussions?" (Journal of Communication, 2014; interviews with commenters plus a content analysis of 1,580 comments): "the discussion factors uncertainty, controversy, comprehensibility, negativity, and personalization" helped explain which comments got replies, and "different technological implementations of the comment function seem to have a limited influence." [University repository record](https://fitforthem.unipa.it/rep:6533b820fe1ef96bd1279858). Per the API summary of the same study, comments at the start of a thread (2.52), comments asking a question (2.18), controversial comments (1.74) and comments addressing someone personally (1.6) were more likely to get feedback, while comments of 10 words or fewer were far less likely to (0.14). [American Press Institute](https://americanpressinstitute.org/?p=3496)
   - Apply: our end question works best when it carries a real open point (uncertainty: what happens next is not known yet) or a real disagreement (two sides, both reasonable), stated so a viewer can understand it without having watched carefully (comprehensibility). Our OWN first comment sits at the top of the thread, which is the spot most likely to get replies: make it a full sentence with one extra verified fact plus a question, not "Thoughts? 👇" (short = ignored).
   - End questions (fill [ ] with verified facts or labelled takes): "Nobody knows yet if [verified open question]. What's your guess, and why?" / "Half of tech says [view A], half says [view B]. Which side are you on?" (only if both views are real and attributed). First comment: "Extra fact: [verified fact + source]. Would this change your plans if you're on OPT?"
3. When a known person replies with praise and questions, comment quality goes up; a logo replying does nothing. Stroud, Scacco, Muddiman & Curry (Engaging News Project, Univ. of Texas; published in Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication, 2015): over 70 days on a US TV station's Facebook page (~2,500 comments), when a well-known reporter took part, the chance of an uncivil comment fell by 15% versus no one taking part; replies from the station's generic logo made no measurable difference. The reporter posted only about 4-5 times a day, praised good comments and asked questions instead of answering uncivil ones. [Journalism.co.uk](https://www.journalism.co.uk/what-happens-when-journalists-interact-with-comments/)
   - Apply: replies should come in the owner's own voice (as Sahil/Harry, the face of the Reel), not as a brand. Praise the specific good point, then ask one follow-up question; skip the trolls. This is about civility and thread quality, not proof of more reach; Instagram has not said how much comment replies count.
   - Reply lines: "Good catch on [their point]. Do you think [follow-up]?" / "That's the part most people miss. Has your company said anything yet?"

**Conflict to flag:** playbook 00 F still lists "Comment TOOLS and I'll DM you the list." Comment-keyword asks are the example Instagram gave of engagement bait it won't recommend (log 11-05). Keep comment-to-DM only for organic replies, or use it rarely and only on posts not meant to reach non-followers; owner's call.

**Our metrics on this** (notes/metrics.md): comment counts are not logged per Reel (the table has shares, saves and likes only), and recent rows say "Replied to 1 comment" / "No unanswered comments", so comment volume is low across the board. No conclusion possible yet.

**Test next:** add a "comments" column to the metrics table. For the next 3 Reels, (a) end on one open question built on a real uncertainty or two-sided disagreement, (b) post our first comment as one full sentence with an extra verified fact plus a question, and (c) reply in the owner's voice with praise + a question. Log comments per reach and how many comments got a reply from another viewer. Watch that sends per reach (~0.42% median, higher recently: Anthropic 1.34%) does not drop, since the comment ask must not replace the send line. A single Reel is not a result.

### 2026-11-15: Originality and "show your receipts": Instagram's repost rules and source labels as trust signals
Source quality: the Instagram rules are platform announcements read through trade press (Engadget 2024; Tubefilter and PetaPixel 2026); Instagram's own blog post was not opened in this run, and no numeric threshold was given for the 2026 change. The source-label result is a 2026 survey experiment by Proof News and Sciences Po (2,000 US adults, randomized), read through Nieman Lab and Editor & Publisher; no effect sizes were published there. Pew is a large probability-based US survey (10,658 adults, Jul-Aug 2024). None of this measures Reels retention directly; it is about recommendability and trust.

**Findings**
1. Instagram stops recommending accounts whose output is mostly other people's content, and crediting the source does not make a repost original. April 2024: aggregators that posted unoriginal Reels "10 or more times in the previous 30 days" lose Reels recommendations; when two identical pieces exist, the original replaces the repost in suggestions; "significant" changes such as voice-overs, reaction clips or posts "materially edited to become a meme" are exempt. [Engadget, Apr 2024](https://www.engadget.com/instagrams-algorithm-overhaul-will-reward-original-content-and-penalize-aggregators-130018977.html). April 2026: the policy was extended from Reels to photos and carousels. Mosseri: "If most of what you post to Instagram is someone else's content, your account is no longer going to be recommendable"; crediting the creator, watermarks, minor crops or speed changes don't qualify; his advice: "remix the content in some way, shape, or form. Make it your own" (own words, commentary, green screen). Status is visible in Account Status, with an appeal. [PetaPixel, Apr 2026](https://petapixel.com/2026/04/30/new-instagram-policies-target-reposted-content/); [Tubefilter, Apr 2026](https://tubefilter.com/2026/04/30/instagram-removes-algorithm-recommendations-repost-content-aggregator)
   - Apply: we are a news page that uses news footage, so we sit close to this line. Every Reel must be visibly OURS: the owner's face and voice, plus a take or a "what this means for you" that the source clip does not have. News B-roll is fine as evidence under his voice; a clip reposted with only a text overlay, or a carousel of screenshots from another page, is not. Check Account Status monthly. This also fits our 11-14 data: the faceless clone-voice Notion Reel flopped (84% skip).
   - Lines that make a clip "ours" (fill [ ] with verified facts or labelled takes): "Everyone's sharing this clip. Here's the part they're missing: [verified detail]." / "That's what [person] said. Here's what it actually means if you're on OPT." / "My take, and I'll label it a take: [opinion]."
2. Showing where the reporting came from raises trust in short news videos, more than in text. Proof News + Sciences Po (2026; 2,000 US adults each shown an unpublished short-form news video with a sources label, a newsroom-standards label, or none): "labeling videos did increase trust — particularly a label that described the video sources in the description"; the same labels on text articles produced fewer significant gains. Sciences Po's Damien Bol on why: "a video forces them to follow the story second by second." Limits: no effect sizes reported in coverage; effects were stronger among Democrats and people with high trust in science. [Nieman Lab, Oct 2026](https://www.niemanlab.org/?p=254599); [Editor & Publisher](https://www.editorandpublisher.com/stories/showing-sources-can-strengthen-trust-in-news-videos-research-finds,264216)
   - Apply: credibility is our asset (log 11-07), and our visa/money facts are exactly what people double-check before sending. (a) In the caption, add a short "Sources:" line naming the primary sources (USCIS notice, SEC filing, company blog, Reuters). (b) On screen, flash the source name for ~1 s at the key fact, on the same shot as the number (a related cut, log 11-12), e.g. "Source: SEC Form 4". (c) Say it once in the voice when it's a primary document.
   - Lines (fill [ ] with verified facts only): "This isn't a rumor. It's in the [verified filing/notice], page [N]." / "Straight from [verified source]: [verified number]." / "I read the actual [notice/filing] so you don't have to. Here's the one line that matters: [verified line]."
3. Young US audiences already get news from people, not newsrooms, and say it helps them understand it. Pew (Jul-Aug 2024, 10,658 US adults): 21% of US adults and 37% of 18-29-year-olds "regularly get news from news influencers"; 65% of those users say news influencers "helped them better understand current events and civic issues"; about seven in ten say this news is "at least somewhat different" from other sources; only 23% of the influencers studied have worked in the news industry. [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/journalism/2024/11/18/americas-news-influencers/)
   - Apply: the value we add is explanation and a personal view, which is also exactly what makes a post "original" under finding 1. Lead with what it means, not just what happened. Since most influencers lack a news background, receipts (finding 2) are how we stand out from them.

### 2026-11-16: Mid-video drop-offs: where people leave after the hook, and how to read the curve to fix the next script
Source quality: Kim et al. is a peer-reviewed log study (ACM Learning at Scale 2014) of second-by-second play/pause data on edX lecture videos (862 videos, 127,839 students, ~39 million events), full text read; it is lecture video, not Reels, so it transfers by analogy. The YouTube Help page is the platform's own documentation (read directly; it covers long-form videos of 60 s+, not Shorts). The Instagram retention/skip-rate quotes are from Instagram's Aug 2025 announcement as quoted by trade press (WeRSM, TechEDT); the like-timing graph and "Link a Reel" are described by Social Samosa without an Instagram quote, so treat their details as secondary.

**Findings**
1. Most leaving happens right at the start, and longer videos lose more people overall. Kim, Guo, Seaton, Mitros, Gajos & Miller, "Understanding In-Video Dropouts and Interaction Peaks in Online Lecture Videos" (L@S 2014): about 55.2% of sessions dropped out before the end, and "36.6% ... occurred within the first 3% of the video length"; "the dropout rate increases with video length" (model: 53% for a 5-minute video vs 71% for 20 minutes). [Kim et al. 2014, PDF (MIT)](https://up.csail.mit.edu/other-pubs/las2014-juhokim-peaks.pdf)
   - Our reading: the drop-off curve is steepest at the very front, then flattens; every extra second of setup is paid for by everyone, every extra second of length by the people who stayed. This backs toolkit 10 and 11 from a different angle, and it fits our 11-15 data: the 69 s Wallet Reel with a slow "So guys, Black Friday is on..." opener held 7.7 s (about 11% of runtime, 72.5% skip), while the ~40-45 s Anthropic IPO and Nvidia Reels held about 20 s (roughly half).
   - Apply: the curve's front is fixed by the hook; the middle is fixed by putting the next reason to stay just BEFORE the point where the last Reel's curve dipped.
2. Re-watch spikes cluster around visual changes and around content that disappeared too fast. Same study: "61% of the categorized peaks involved a visual transition before, and/or after the peak," and in 23% of peaks "students return to visual content that disappears shortly after." Re-watching sessions made about twice as many peaks as first views (2.2 vs 1.0). YouTube's own definition agrees on the double meaning: spikes are moments "rewatched or shared," which can mean the audience valued it or that "your content isn't clear and your audience had to rewatch a section." [YouTube Help: Measure key moments for audience retention](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415?hl=en)
   - Apply: a bump on our retention chart is good news only if the moment is the key fact we wanted saved. If it sits on a number card that flashed for under a second, the card was too fast (keep it on screen ~2 s, toolkit 5). If it sits on a confusing line, rewrite that kind of line next time.
   - Lines for a clear, hold-on-screen key fact (fill [ ] with verified facts only): "Pause on this one. [verified number]. That's the whole story." / "Write this date down: [verified date]."
3. The platforms tell creators to read dips and "top moments" and move the best part earlier. YouTube: dips are moments "either skipped" or where "viewers stopped watching your video completely," to be reviewed "to better understand why audiences lost interest"; top moments are where "almost no one dropped off," and if they come late, YouTube recommends "introducing the compelling content earlier in the video." Instagram (Aug 2025, as quoted by trade press): Retention "shows you the moments in your Reel that keep or lose your viewers' interest"; "you'll always see a downward slope, but the flatter the line, the more engaged your audience is"; skip rate is "the percentage of views from people who decided to skip your Reel during those first 3 seconds." [WeRSM](https://wersm.com/instagram-adds-retention-metrics-for-reels). The same Aug 2025 update added a Reel like-timing graph (when in the Reel viewers tap like) and "Link a Reel" for series (a "Watch Part 2" button). [Social Samosa](https://www.socialsamosa.com/topical-spot/instagram-new-engagement-tools-explained-9762802)
   - Apply (a post-mortem in 2 minutes per Reel, from Insights on day 2): (1) the second of the steepest drop AFTER 3 s, and the line spoken there; (2) the second where likes peak (that is our "top moment"); (3) any bump. Next script: put a re-commit or open loop 1-2 s before the dip type we saw (often the switch from hook to background context), and move the top-moment type of line earlier, ideally inside the first 10 s, while still ending on a usable peak (toolkit 6). For part-2 Reels, use Link a Reel instead of a spoken "part 2" demand.
   - Re-commit lines for the hook-to-context switch, where background usually starts (fill [ ] with verified facts only): "That's what happened. Here's what it does to your [paycheck / OPT clock]." / "The headline is [verified fact]. The part that matters to you is one sentence away." / "Skip the backstory, here's the only bit you need: [verified fact]."

**Our metrics on this** (notes/metrics.md): we log skip rate and average watch, but not the retention-chart dip second, the like peak, or runtime on every row, so mid-video dips cannot be checked yet. The one clean contrast (Wallet 69 s, 7.7 s avg vs Anthropic/Nvidia ~40-45 s, ~20 s avg) fits finding 1, but topic and opener also differed; directional only.

**Test next:** add three columns to the metrics table: runtime, "biggest dip after 3 s (sec + line)" and "like peak (sec)". For the next 3 Reels, place a re-commit line 1-2 s before the second where the previous Reel's biggest dip happened, and put the line type that drew the like peak inside the first 10 s. Compare avg watch time as % of runtime (target above Anthropic/Nvidia's roughly 45-50%), check that the new dip is shallower or later, and keep watching skip rate against ~60%. A single Reel is not a result.

### 2026-11-17: Big numbers: how people misread millions, billions and trillions, and how to make the key number land
Source quality: Barrio, Goldstein & Hofman is a peer-reviewed CHI 2016 paper (three randomized online experiments, 3,200+ people, numbers taken from real news articles); I read the abstract on Microsoft Research, plus one example sentence via a 2018 Aalto thesis that summarises it. Landy, Silbert & Goldin is peer-reviewed (Cognitive Science, 2013), read via its PhilArchive abstract and a Nautilus summary. Jerez-Fernandez, Angulo & Oppenheimer (Psychological Science, 2014) is read via the APS news write-up and a Wikipedia summary (the two quote slightly different example numbers, so only the direction is used). Henderson et al. is a peer-reviewed preregistered replication (full text read). All are text or lab tasks, not Reels, so they transfer by analogy. Our winners are money stories ($1.4T Anthropic, -$190B Nvidia, "this week decides AI stocks"), so this applies to most of our scripts.

**Findings**
1. Half of adults squash millions, billions and trillions together. Landy, Silbert & Goldin, "Estimating Large Numbers" (Cognitive Science, 2013): "numbers in the range of 1 million to 1 trillion are notoriously difficult to understand." About half of US adults placed large numbers on a number line correctly; nearly all the others put 1 million about midway between 1 thousand and 1 billion, as if the number words were evenly spaced (so 980 million and 2 billion felt close). Writing out the zeros or using powers of ten did not help. [PhilArchive](https://dc2.philarchive.org/rec/LANELN); [Nautilus summary](https://nautil.us/how-to-understand-extreme-numbers-236443)
   - Apply: "$190 billion" and "$1.4 trillion" are both just "huge" to about half our viewers; the size difference, which is often the actual story, is lost. Never put two numbers with different words (million vs billion, billion vs trillion) side by side and expect the viewer to feel the gap. Convert to ONE unit (e.g. our own two cover figures: "$1,400 billion vs $190 billion") or use a ratio (1,400 ÷ 190 = about 7 times), checked by calculator.
   - Lines (fill [ ] with verified facts only): "That's not billion. That's trillion, with a T: about [verified ratio] times [verified company] today." / "Nvidia lost [verified figure] in one day. That's more than [verified comparison company]'s entire value."
2. One short comparison sentence ("perspective") makes a news number stick, and helps people catch wrong numbers. Barrio, Goldstein & Hofman, "Improving Comprehension of Numbers in the News" (CHI 2016; three randomized experiments, 3,200+ participants): perspectives are "simple sentences ... that employ ratios, ranks, and unit changes"; "perspective clauses substantially improve people's ability to recall measurements they have read, estimate ones they have not, and detect errors in manipulated measurements." Example form: "3,400 miles is about 1.27 times larger than the width of the continental United States." [Microsoft Research](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=215388); [Aalto thesis citing it](https://core.ac.uk/download/159158671.pdf)
   - Apply: every Reel's ONE key number gets ONE perspective sentence right after it, using a ratio, a rank or a unit change, anchored to something our viewer already knows (a company they know, their own salary, an H-1B fee, an iPhone, India's or a US state's budget). Recall is what we want: a number they remember is a number they repeat at work (North Star) and send. This is also the natural content for the [slow] beat and the ~2 s held shot (toolkit 5): number, pause, perspective.
   - Rank form is the cheapest and hardest to get wrong: "That would make it the [verified rank] most valuable company on Earth." Unit change: "That's about $[verified figure] for every person in the US." Ratio: "[verified figure] is roughly [verified ratio] times what [verified company] made all last year."
3. Exact numbers sound more confident than round ones. Jerez-Fernandez, Angulo & Oppenheimer, "Show Me the Numbers: Precision as a Cue to Others' Confidence" (Psychological Science, 2014): people read a precise estimate as coming from a more confident source than a round one of equal accuracy, and in an advice game they "clearly preferred the more precise advisors." The APS write-up adds the caveat: "this all has to do with perceptions of confidence—not true expertise." [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/were-only-human/precisely-right-no-doubt-trust-me.html); [DOI 10.1177/0956797613504301](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797613504301)
   - Apply: in the ONE key number, say the figure exactly as the primary source gives it ("$[exact figure] billion" rather than "around two hundred billion") and show the source tag (toolkit 15); round only the perspective sentence ("about [N] times"). Never add false precision: if the source says "about $1.4 trillion", we say "about $1.4 trillion". Precision we invent would be a factual error that a confident tone makes worse.
4. Softening an old belief: "concrete wording makes a claim sound truer" did not replicate. Henderson et al., preregistered replication of Hansen & Wänke (2010) (2021, Kingston University eprint of the published paper; 246 + 220 participants analysed): effects dz = 0.08 and 0.11 vs the original 0.48 (n = 46); "these results do not provide evidence for a linguistic concreteness effect on truth judgements." [Kingston eprints](https://eprints.kingston.ac.uk/id/eprint/43028/1/Henderson-E-L-43028-VoR.pdf)
   - Apply: concrete numbers and comparisons help because people UNDERSTAND and REMEMBER them (finding 2), not because concrete words make people believe us. Trust still has to come from receipts (source tag, caption "Sources:" line), not from vivid phrasing.

**Our metrics on this** (notes/metrics.md): number framing is not logged. Our top three day-1 Reels all centre on one big money number (Anthropic IPO "$1.4T?", Nvidia "-$190B in a day", Week stocks: dated earnings/IPO), which fits "money at stake", but none of their scripts had a deliberate perspective sentence, so we have no contrast yet. The 11-15 "Week stocks" Reel had record shares (43) with 11.0 s avg watch (lower than Anthropic/Nvidia's ~20 s), so a clearer key-number beat may be one place to win back watch time.

**Test next:** for the next 3 money/number Reels, (a) say the key number exactly as the source gives it, (b) follow it with ONE perspective sentence (rank, ratio or unit change, calculator-checked) during the held shot, and (c) never compare numbers across million/billion/trillion without converting to one unit. Log "perspective line? y/n" and the line itself in each metrics row. Compare saves per reach against the recent day-1 level (Anthropic 0.51%, Week stocks 12/2,213 = 0.54%) and sends per reach (Week stocks 43/2,213 = 1.94%), and check the retention chart for a bump or flat stretch at the number beat rather than a drop. A single Reel is not a result.

### 2026-11-18: One person vs the statistic: identifiable cases, exemplars and stories in news
Source quality: Small, Loewenstein & Slovic (OBHDP 2007) is read via Stanford GSB's write-up (no exact dollar figures given there) and Wikipedia's summary; Kogut & Ritov (2005) and Lee & Feeley's meta-analysis (Social Influence 2016) via Wikipedia's summary, not the papers. Lindauer et al. (Judgment and Decision Making 2020, N = 975) full PDF read. Braddock & Dillard's meta-analysis (Communication Monographs 2016, 74 studies) via The Decision Lab's summary. Sellnow-Richmond et al. (Frontiers in Communication 2024, N = 238 students) full text read. Ecker, Butler & Hamby (Cognitive Research 2020, three preregistered experiments, N = 2,279) via the DOAJ abstract. RISJ "Understanding Young News Audiences" (March 2026) via the Oxford DPIR news post. Most of this is charity/text research, not Reels; it transfers by analogy.

**Findings**
1. One named person moves people more than a number, but the effect is small and fragile. Small, Loewenstein & Slovic (2007): people gave much more to one starving girl ("Rokia") than to a famine described with statistics; when statistics were added next to Rokia, "overall donations actually decline" ("Insight, in this situation, seems to breed callousness"). [Stanford GSB](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/increase-charitable-donations-appeal-heart-not-head). Kogut & Ritov (2005): identifying ONE child raised donations, identifying each of a group of eight did not ("singularity effect"). Lee & Feeley's meta-analysis: "the effect size, in general, is small," larger when a photograph is shown and when the person is not seen as responsible for their plight. [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identifiable_victim_effect). Softener: Lindauer et al. (2020) found a single-child emotional appeal and a reasoned argument raised giving about equally ($45.51 vs $41.83 vs $35.66 control; emotional vs rational not significant), and adding the argument did not dampen the emotional appeal. [JDM PDF](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/C39B98F3AB2ABB03A7055A013BE2C899/S1930297500007208a.pdf/comparing-the-effect-of-rational-and-emotional-appeals-on-donation-behavior.pdf)
   - Our reading: one real case is a cheap boost to the stop and the feeling, not a magic switch. ONE person beats "thousands of workers"; a face/photo of that person helps; and the number does not have to fight the story if it comes as one perspective line (toolkit 9), not a pile of stats.
   - Apply: when a story has a real, publicly reported person (a named founder, a laid-off engineer who posted publicly, a CEO), open the stake on them, then widen to "you". Never invent or composite a person; use only someone the source names, and show the source.
   - Lines (fill [ ] with verified facts only): "[Verified name] found out on [verified day] that [verified fact]. If you're on H-1B, the same rule applies to you." / "This is [verified name]. [verified number] of people are in the same spot. Here's what they can do."
2. Examples beat base rates for attention and perceived importance, but base rates are trusted more, and an atypical example misleads. Exemplification research (Zillmann and colleagues): concrete examples usually outweigh statistics when people judge how common something is, while base rates are seen as more "truthful, precise, and accurate." [Wikipedia: Exemplification theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exemplification_theory). Sellnow-Richmond et al. (2024): news with exemplars was rated more interesting than the base-rate version: "the use of exemplars in news stories does increase readers' perception that the content is important." [Frontiers](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2024.1316677/full)
   - Our reading: the example earns the attention; the number earns the trust. Pair them: case first, then the real base rate so the viewer doesn't think the extreme case is normal.
   - Apply (factual-safe): if the example is unusual, say so ("this is the extreme case; the typical one is [verified figure]"). An exaggerated example in a visa or layoffs story would mislead an anxious audience and costs trust (toolkit 15).
   - Line: "That's the worst case. For most people on OPT, the number is [verified figure], and here's how to check yours."
3. Stories help a little; they don't replace clear information. Braddock & Dillard's meta-analysis of 74 narrative-vs-non-narrative studies: "a small but meaningful influence" (beliefs r ≈ .17, behaviours ≈ .23), with nonfiction narratives moving beliefs. [The Decision Lab](https://thedecisionlab.com/insights/society/can-stories-change-us). But Ecker, Butler & Hamby (2020): "narrative corrections are no more effective than non-narrative corrections" in all three experiments. [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/ae832539265f47ab8f765d3910d71b91)
   - Apply: use a person as the 2-5 s doorway, not as the whole Reel. When we are correcting a rumour ("No, OPT isn't being cancelled tomorrow"), a plain clear statement + source works as well as a story; don't spend 15 s of setup on a narrative.
4. Young news audiences follow personalities and want news that is easier, more relevant and "less gloomy." RISJ, "Understanding Young News Audiences at a Time of Rapid Change" (March 2026, synthesis of Digital News Report data 2013-2025): 18-24s pay more attention to individual creators than news brands; about 42% sometimes or often avoid news because it seems irrelevant, depressing or hard to follow. [Oxford DPIR](https://www.politics.ox.ac.uk/news/young-audiences-are-redefining-how-they-consume-news-new-reuters-report-argues)
   - Apply: the owner IS the "one person" in every Reel (toolkit 11, 15). His reaction is the human element when a story has no named case: "I read the filing so you don't have to. One line in it changes [verified thing] for you." Keep the "what you can do" turn (toolkit 2) so people-stories never end gloomy.

**Our metrics on this** (notes/metrics.md): "named person in hook" is not logged. Our best real day-1 result (11-15 Week stocks, 2,213 reach, 43 shares) had no single person (famous tickers + dated stakes); Nvidia/Jensen (a famous person) held best (~20 s) but had fewer sends (10/1,582). So a person is NOT required for a winner; treat this as an optional boost, not a rule. 11-14 to 11-17 sends/likes are polluted by the paid-service bot activity; use reach, saves, avg watch and skip rate only for those rows.

**Test next:** for the next 3 Reels where a real, source-named person exists, open the stake on that ONE person (photo or clip on screen from 0.0 s, beside the owner), then widen to "you" by second 5, then the base-rate number with a perspective line. Log "named case in hook? y/n" in each metrics row. Compare skip rate against the ~50-55% recent day-1 level (Week stocks 52.9%, Anthropic 50.3%) and avg watch against ~18-20 s (Anthropic/Nvidia); use saves per reach (Week stocks 0.54%) rather than shares while bot activity continues. A single Reel is not a result.

### 2026-11-19: Timing and triggers: why dated, "this week" stories keep getting talked about, and how fast news goes stale
Source quality: Berger & Schwartz is a peer-reviewed paper (Journal of Marketing Research, 2011), full PDF read (Wharton); its data is product word of mouth (BzzAgent campaigns), not Reels. Berger & Fitzsimons (JMR, 2008) is read through Knowledge at Wharton's write-up, not the paper. Castillo et al. is a peer-reviewed ACM paper (DOI 10.1145/2531602.2531623, CSCW 2014 proceedings per the DOI) on one international news site's articles, read via arXiv/ar5iv. The Instagram quotes are from Instagram's own "Instagram Ranking Explained" post (Mosseri, May 2023), read directly; it is from 2023 and says nothing about how much each signal weighs. None of this measures Reels retention; it is about what gets talked about and when.

**Findings**
1. Interesting content gets talked about right away; content that the world keeps CUING gets talked about for longer. Berger & Schwartz, "What Drives Immediate and Ongoing Word of Mouth?" (JMR 2011; ~11,000 observations across 300+ products/335 campaigns, a field experiment with 1,687 people, and a lab study with 120): more interesting products "do not receive more ongoing WOM over a multimonth period (or overall)," while "Products that are cued more by the environment receive more immediate WOM," and cues and public visibility kept working over time. In the field test, linking Boston Market to dinner "boosted WOM by 20%" among people who did not already make that link. [Berger & Schwartz 2011, PDF (Wharton)](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/10/BzzAgent.pdf)
   - Our reading: "wow, interesting" gets the first-day send; what keeps a Reel being sent and talked about at work is a TRIGGER the viewer meets again: an earnings call on Thursday, an IPO's first trading day, an OPT or H-1B deadline, payday, the moment they open ChatGPT at work. This fits our best real day-1 Reel (11-15 Week stocks: dated "this Tue/Thu" stakes, 43 shares on 2,213 reach) and Midterms (posted on election day, 10 shares, then our record).
   - Apply: attach every story to ONE dated or recurring cue the viewer will hit again, and say it out loud. Only use dates that are verified.
   - Lines (fill [ ] with verified facts only): "Thursday after the bell, [verified company] reports. Remember this number when you see the headline: [verified figure]." / "Next time you open ChatGPT at work, remember this: [verified fact]." / "If you're on OPT, [verified date] is the day this starts. Put it in your calendar."
2. Cues work even when people don't notice them, and repeated cues work better. Berger & Fitzsimons, "Dogs on the Street, Pumas on Your Feet" (JMR 2008): shoppers asked the day before Halloween were "almost twice as likely to list orange-colored products" as a week later (144 shoppers); people shown more dog pictures recognised the Puma brand about 30% faster; the authors say priming "can occur outside of conscious awareness," and frequency of exposure matters, not only recency. [Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/?p=7063)
   - Our reading (lab and shopping studies, analogy only): a recurring format with the same cue ("every earnings Thursday", "Monday: what changed for visas this week") becomes its own trigger, so viewers start to expect and look for it. This backs the owner's Tue/Thu after-the-bell promise: keep the same day, same frame-1 wording, same opening line shape.
   - Frame-1 text for a recurring slot (≤5 words): "Earnings Thursday: [ticker]" / "Visa week: what changed".
3. News loses attention within hours, and early engagement speed is a signal Instagram uses. Castillo, El-Haddad, Pfeffer & Stempeck, "Characterizing the Life Cycle of Online News Stories Using Social Media Reactions" (ACM, 2014; 3M+ visits, 200k social reactions): about 78% of news articles had traffic that "decreases monotonically during the first 12 hours"; the average half-life was 8 hours for news and 20 hours for in-depth pieces; social reactions in the first 10-20 minutes predicted later traffic about as well as 3 hours of visits. [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1304.3010). Instagram's own explainer lists "when a post was shared" among Feed signals, and for Explore counts "how quickly other people are liking, commenting, sharing, and saving a post"; for Reels the key predictions are how likely you are "to reshare a reel, watch a reel all the way through, like it, and go to the audio page." [Instagram, May 2023](https://about.instagram.com/blog/announcements/instagram-ranking-explained)
   - Apply: for breaking stories (IPO day, earnings, a visa rule), a good Reel posted within a few hours beats a perfect one posted the next day; the explainer angle ("what it means for you") keeps a longer shelf life than pure "X happened", which fits our 20-hour vs 8-hour reading. For in-depth evergreen pieces (how-tos, PAIN→FIX), timing matters less. Post when our audience is awake (playbook 00 E: 7-9 AM or 5-7 PM PT) so the first hour gets real, fast reactions. Never buy or fake that early engagement (our 11-14 to 11-18 bot pattern shows it only pollutes our data).
   - Lines for a same-day Reel (fill [ ] with verified facts only): "[Verified company] started trading [verified hours] ago. Here's the one number that matters: [verified figure]." / "This dropped this morning. If you're on H-1B, here's what changes: [verified change]."

**Our metrics on this** (notes/metrics.md): posting delay after the news and "trigger named? y/n" are not logged. The two dated, on-the-day Reels (Week stocks, Midterms) are our top sharers, and Week stocks kept growing to day 3 (2,875 reach), which fits findings 1-2; but both were also money/visa stakes with face hooks, so directional only. 11-14 to 11-18 shares/likes are bot-polluted; judge by reach, saves, avg watch and skip rate.

**Test next:** for the next 3 news Reels, (a) name ONE verified dated or recurring cue in the script ("Thursday after the bell", "starts [date]") and repeat it in the last line, and (b) log "hours from news to post" and "trigger named? y/n" in each metrics row. Compare reach growth from day 1 to day 3 (Week stocks: 2,213 → 2,896) and saves per reach (Week stocks 0.54%) against Reels without a dated cue; for the Tue/Thu slot, check whether day-1 reach rises across consecutive weeks. A single Reel is not a result.

### 2026-11-20: Saves: what makes people keep a Reel (practical value, "offloading" to a save, and what Instagram says saves count for)
Source quality: Berger & Milkman (Journal of Marketing Research 2012) is a peer-reviewed field study of 6,956 NYT home-page articles (Aug-Nov 2008), PDF read on Wharton's site (first half verbatim; the emotion side was logged 11-09, today uses only the practical-value control). Mosseri's words come through German trade press (OnlineMarketing.de/t3n, 03 Feb 2025 and 27 May 2026) quoting his AMA and a Threads video; only short phrases are his, the rest is the editors' paraphrase, so treat the saves part as unverified. Storm & Stone (Psychological Science 2015, 26(2), 182-188; three small lab studies with undergrads and word lists in PDF files) read via the APS press release and BPS Research Digest; a 2023 preregistered replication (Tsai et al., Applied Cognitive Psychology, n = 102 + 88, UCL PDF) found it only partly. None of this measures Reels; the memory work transfers by analogy. A decodeinternet.in piece on why young Indians save posts could not be fetched.

**Findings**
1. Useful content travels about as well as awe-inducing content, independent of emotion. In Berger & Milkman's full model, human-rated "practical utility" predicted making the NYT most-emailed list (logit coefficient .34, p < .001; awe was also .34, anger .38) while controlling for emotion, home-page placement and time, length and author. Their summary: emotion effects hold "even when the authors control for how surprising, interesting, or practically useful content is (all of which are positively linked to virality)," because "practically useful content may be more viral because it provides information." [Berger & Milkman 2012, PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/11/Virality.pdf)
   - Our reading: a "here's what to do / check / watch" segment is not a boring add-on; in this data usefulness carries about as much weight as awe. It is also the part people come back to, which is what a save is.
   - Apply: every Reel has ONE concrete reusable item: a date, a 3-step checklist, a list of tickers and report days, the exact form or setting name. Make it specific enough that someone would need to look it up again.
2. Instagram says it weights likes more than saves mainly because likes are more plentiful; saves are at most a supporting signal. Mosseri (AMA, reported 03 Feb 2025): "we look at likes more because there are a lot more likes"; earlier: "sends per reach correlate more, in my experience, with overall reach than anything else." The article's summary of his early-2025 list: watch time, likes per reach and sends per reach decide; saves "may also play a role." In May 2026 he told creators: "The thing that matters most that you should focus on is not how much reach your post got," with the editors adding likes, comments, saves, shares and sends as the quality signals. [OnlineMarketing.de, Feb 2025](https://onlinemarketing.de/social-media-marketing/warum-instagram-weiterhin-likes-fokussiert) / [t3n, 2026](https://t3n.de/news/reels-instagram-bewerten-mosseri-1744320/)
   - Our reading: don't trade the send for the save. A save is our cleanest REAL signal right now (bots pump shares and likes, not saves), so it's the best quality check, but the ranking levers stay watch time, sends and likes (toolkit 8).
3. People "offload" what they save, and they only offload what they trust will still be there. Storm & Stone: students who SAVED a first word list remembered more of a second list than when they just closed the file; the benefit vanished when saving was made unreliable ("files kept getting lost") or when the first list was too small to strain memory. [APS release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/saving-old-information-can-boost-memory-for-new-information.html) / [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/saving-information-computer-frees-your-mind-learn-new-material). Replication (Tsai et al. 2023, preregistered, n = 102): the effect appeared only in one test order (d = .46 vs .07 in the other). [UCL PDF](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10169254/1/An%20investigation%20of%20the%20saving%20enhanced%20memory%20effect.pdf)
   - Our reading (lab analogy, effect fragile): a save is a "remember this for me" move. It only makes sense when the Reel holds MORE than the viewer can remember (several dates, steps or numbers) and when the saved Reel will show that list clearly when they come back. One short fact gets remembered or sent, not saved.
   - Apply: put the reusable item on ONE clean "save card" frame (max 3 lines, readable when paused, held ~2 s, same shot as toolkit 5's hold) and say why they'll need it later. Ask for the save only on Reels that have such a card; never on a pure opinion or one-fact Reel.
   - Lines (fill [ ] with verified facts only): "Three dates if you're on OPT. You won't remember all of them, so save this: [date 1], [date 2], [date 3]." / "Here's the checklist I'd use before [verified event]. Screenshot it or save it, it's on screen for two seconds." / "Earnings this week: [ticker] [day], [ticker] [day]. Save it and check back Thursday after the bell."

**Our metrics on this** (notes/metrics.md, saves per reach, the least bot-polluted engagement): A16Z 2/316 = 0.63% (tiny reach), Week stocks 14/2,908 = 0.48% (a dated list of tickers and days: fits finding 3), Anthropic IPO 8/1,855 = 0.43%, Outage 4/1,229 = 0.33%, Nvidia/Jensen 2/1,582 = 0.13% (one-company story, no list). The Anthropic carousel had 0 saves on 494 reach despite 32 "shares" (likely inflated). Directional only; "save card? y/n" is not logged.

**Test next:** for the next 3 news Reels that have 3+ verified dates, steps or tickers, add ONE "save card" frame (≤3 lines, held ~2 s) plus a single spoken save line tied to a later moment ("check back Thursday"); skip the save ask on one-fact Reels. Log "save card? y/n" in each metrics row. Compare saves per reach on day 2 against the Week stocks level (0.48%) and our recent average without a list (~0.3%); check avg watch doesn't drop (the card must not stall the pace). A single Reel is not a result.

### 2026-11-21: Saying how sure you are: uncertainty, "my theory" framing and corrections vs trust
Source quality: van der Bles et al. (PNAS 2020, five UK experiments incl. a BBC News field test, n = 5,780) read via the EurekAlert press release and an AI Impacts summary (secondary; exact effect sizes not read). Tenney et al. (Psychological Science 2007, two small lab studies, n = 48 and 103, courtroom transcripts) read via BPS Research Digest. Nyhan et al. (Journal of Experimental Political Science 2023, n = 2,862, simulated Twitter feed, CBC) read via a Nieman Lab commentary, size of the trust drop not reported. None of this measures Reels; it transfers by analogy to a news creator's voice.

**Findings**
1. Giving a number's uncertainty as a range barely costs trust; vague verbal hedging costs more. van der Bles et al.: numeric uncertainty "resulted in a small decrease in the trust of that number" and "no significant change in the trust of the source"; verbal uncertainty ("there is some uncertainty") gave "a larger decrease in the trust of that number" and "a small decrease in the trust of the source." Prior beliefs mattered more than format. [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/pub_releases/2020-03/potn-pta031820.php) / [AI Impacts summary](https://blog.aiimpacts.org/p/paper-summary-the-effects-of-communicating) / [Cambridge repository](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/handle/1810/304699)
   - Apply: when a source gives an estimate or range (analyst targets, "between X and Y", "up to"), say the range the source gives, not "nobody really knows" or "it could be a lot." Hedge with the source's numbers, not with filler words ("maybe", "kind of", "some say").
   - Lines (fill [ ] with verified facts only): "[Source] says between [low] and [high]. Even the low end is [perspective line]." / "The filing says up to [N]. Up to, not exactly, so here's the floor."
2. Confidence wins only until you're wrong; then the calibrated speaker is trusted more. Tenney et al.: a confident witness was rated more credible until shown in an error, after which "the confident version of the witness was judged less credible than the uncertain version"; "confident witnesses who make mistakes are perceived to be the least reliable of all." Summary: people "are deemed trustworthy when their confidence matches their accuracy." [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/courtroom-confidence-backfires-when-witness-makes-error) / [Stanford Law record](https://law.stanford.edu/?p=13107)
   - Our reading: keep two clear voices. Verified facts are said flat and confident (fits toolkit 5/9). Predictions and takes are labelled as ours, with the reason, so a miss costs a theory, not the account. Our 11-18 Outage Reel was framed "my theory" and had our longest avg watch on a long Reel (12.9 s on 69 s) and 42 shares: consistent with this, not proof.
   - Lines: "That's the fact. Here's my theory, and I could be wrong: [take], because [verified reason]." / "What's confirmed: [fact]. What's not: [open point]. I'll update you [verified date]." (the second also feeds toolkit 14's real-uncertainty comment question)
3. Correcting yourself fixes beliefs better than anyone else can, but it lowers trust in general, so prevention is the main lever. Nyhan et al.: people corrected by the outlet itself "had a better understanding of the actual facts than those who had seen a correction posted by a third party", but "no matter who posted the correction... participants were afterward less likely to say they trusted the news outlet's reporting." [Nieman Lab](https://niemanlab.org/2023/03/the-corrections-dilemma-admitting-your-mistakes-increases-accuracy-but-reduces-audience-trust-a-new-study-finds) / [paper](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-experimental-political-science/article/corrections-dilemma-media-retractions-increase-belief-accuracy-but-decrease-trust/A6A9A16D97F22E1CA22B051D215E752B)
   - Apply: the trust cost is a reason to fact-check harder before posting, never a reason to skip a correction. If a Reel has a real error, we post the correction ourselves (pinned comment + caption edit, and a short follow-up Reel if the error changed the takeaway), since our own correction informs better than a commenter's.
   - Pinned-comment line: "Correction: I said [wrong], it's actually [right] per [source]. The point still stands / changes like this: [one line]."

**Test next:** for the next 3 Reels that include a prediction or take, script it as "Fact: … / My theory: … because …" with the source's range where there is one, and no filler hedges. Compare avg watch time and sends per reach against the Outage Reel (12.9 s, 42 sends / 1,282 reach) and our ~0.42% sends median, and log comments per reach (a stated open point should draw replies). Log "theory-labelled? y/n" per row. A single Reel is not a result.

### 2026-11-22: From viewer to follower: credibility, "people like me", familiarity from a recurring format, and posting rhythm
Source quality: Han & Balabanis (Psychology & Marketing 2023) is a peer-reviewed meta-analysis (250 effect sizes, 58 studies, N = 25,080) of influencer studies, mostly surveys about brand attitudes and purchase intention, not follows; abstract and conclusion read verbatim on City Research Online, the r values below come from a read of the results section that I could not check against Table 1, so treat them as approximate. Bornstein (Psychological Bulletin 1989, 208 experiments) and Montoya et al. (Psychological Bulletin 2017) are read only through Wikipedia's summary; they are lab studies of repeated stimuli (faces, words, songs), not creators. The Instagram items are Meta's own Trial Reels post (Dec 2024, read directly) and Mosseri as quoted or paraphrased by Social Media Today and RouteNote (no posts-per-week number exists there; the frequency point is the writer's paraphrase). Our follower count is polluted by the paid service (11-17 to 11-19), so follower totals cannot judge any Reel.

**Findings**
1. What makes people take an influencer seriously is credibility first, then "they're like me"; content being informative matters less. Han & Balabanis: "Source Credibility Theory emerges as the most robust explanatory framework," "highlighting the significance of credibility, trustworthiness, and perceived expertise"; behavioral outcomes showed "moderate effects ... for homophily" (similarity to the viewer). Approximate r: credibility → attitude toward the influencer ≈ .73, trustworthiness → attitude ≈ .60, homophily → purchase intention ≈ .51, expertise ≈ .40, informativeness → purchase intention ≈ .29. [Han & Balabanis 2023, PDF (City, Univ. of London)](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/31583/1/Psychology%20and%20Marketing%20-%202023%20-%20Han%20-%20Meta%E2%80%90analysis%20of%20social%20media%20influencer%20impact%20Key%20antecedents%20and%20theoretical.pdf)
   - Our reading: a follow is a bet that the NEXT Reel will also be worth it. Viewers make that bet on the person (is he right, is he one of us), not on one good fact. This backs toolkit 15 (receipts, calibrated takes) and adds one thing we under-use: honest, specific similarity. The owner works in and around US tech and speaks to the same worries; saying so, once and briefly, is the homophily cue.
   - Apply (factual-safe): only state similarity that is literally true for the owner; never claim a visa status, employer or salary he doesn't have. One line max, then back to the story.
   - Lines: "I read these filings every week so you don't have to." / "I work in tech too, so here's the part I'd actually check first: [verified item]." (only if true) / "I said [past take] on [date]. Here's how that's going: [verified update]." (a track record is the strongest credibility cue; use only when the earlier take is on our page)
2. Familiarity grows liking, but only up to a point, then repetition wears out. Bornstein's meta-analysis of 208 experiments: the mere-exposure effect is "robust and reliable, with an effect size of r=0.26"; it is "strongest when unfamiliar stimuli are presented briefly" and "typically reaches its maximum effect within 10–20 presentations"; with many more repetitions liking can turn down (inverted U, Montoya et al. 2017). For advertising the evidence is "mixed." [Wikipedia: Mere-exposure effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mere-exposure_effect)
   - Our reading (lab analogy): the same face, frame-1 layout, colour palette and opening line shape across Reels lets a non-follower recognise us on the 2nd-5th time we appear in their feed, which is when a follow becomes likely. Repeating the same STORY or the same hook line does the opposite (wear-out). So: same wrapper, new content.
   - Apply: run 2-3 named recurring series with a fixed day and fixed frame-1 wording (e.g. the owner's Tue/Thu after-the-bell slot, "Who's hiring" from 11-16), and say the series name in the first 2 s. Rotate the share-trigger line and hook formula inside it (playbook 0 already says never the same trigger twice in a row).
   - Lines: "Earnings Thursday, after the bell: [ticker] just reported [verified figure]." / "Who's hiring this week: [verified company] wants a [verified role]." / last line of a series Reel: "Every Thursday after the bell, one number that matters. Today's was [verified figure]." (a reason to follow without saying "follow")
3. Instagram's own levers: most reach comes from non-followers, posting more gets more reach, and risky ideas can be tested away from followers. Mosseri: "There's way more unconnected content ... than there is connected content" (Social Media Today, Jun 2024). [Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-chief-shares-insights-into-its-algorithms-creator-monetization/718540/). On frequency he gave no number ("one thing a month" to "five Reels a day" as examples); RouteNote's paraphrase: more posts generally mean more reach, but pick a schedule you can sustain without burnout (Dec 2024). [RouteNote](https://routenote.com/blog/how-often-you-should-post-on-instagram/). Trial Reels (Meta, Dec 2024): "Trial reels will be shown to non-followers first"; after about 24 h the creator sees views, likes, comments and shares, and can "automatically share your trial reel with followers" if it does well; Instagram built it because creators feared posting off-niche content "for fear of it not resonating with their followers." [Meta Newsroom](https://about.fb.com/news/2024/12/trial-reels-try-content-non-followers-first-see-what-perfoms-best)
   - Apply: a steady daily rhythm the owner can keep beats bursts and gaps (our 11-19 gap = +9 followers, see below). Use Trial Reels for format experiments (a new series, a pop-culture angle, a different hook type) so a miss doesn't hit the followers' feed, then let the good ones auto-share. ### 2026-11-23: Background music under the voice: when a music bed helps, when it costs understanding, and how loud it can be
Source quality: Moreno & Mayer is a peer-reviewed pair of lab experiments (Journal of Educational Psychology 2000, 92(1), 117-125; n = 75 + 75 students, one bland synthesized music loop, narrated science animations), full PDF read; the Exp 2 numbers came from a summary of the PDF's tail, so only Exp 1 is quoted. Scharenborg & van Os is a narrative review of about 23 lab studies (TU Delft preprint, 2019), only the first ~40% read verbatim. The broadcast numbers are a 2011 trade-blog report of a BBC survey (8,000 online panel adults + 508 over-65s) and the BBC "3 dB / no lyrics" guidance as quoted in a University of Salford dataset record; the 10 LU figure is Torcoli et al. as cited inside Geary et al. (JAES 2020), not read first-hand. None of this is Reels data, and none of it measured scroll behaviour; it is about whether people understand and remember the words. Context: playbook rules conflict on music (11-03 "music under the whole Reel" vs 11-10 "no added music on the owner's self-recorded Reels unless he asks"); the owner's 11-10 rule stands, this entry is about every Reel that DOES get a bed (Harry/clone, text-led, or owner asks).

**Findings**
1. A music bed under narration cut what people remembered and could use. Moreno & Mayer, "A Coherence Effect in Multimedia Learning: The Case for Minimizing Irrelevant Sounds" (Exp 1, lightning explainer, n = 75): students "remembered significantly less verbal material when music had been presented (M = 7.65 ...) than when no music had been presented (M = 11.37 ...)," and "generated significantly fewer solutions when music had been presented (M = 1.54) than when no music had been presented (M = 2.84)." Sound effects alone did not hurt retention (p = .13); music + sound effects together "was particularly detrimental." Our math: about a third less recalled with music. Limits: one "synthesized and bland" loop, students in a lab, a learning test rather than a scroll. [Moreno & Mayer 2000, PDF (Univ. of Geneva)](https://tecfa.unige.ch/tecfa/teaching/methodo/Moreno_Mayer00.pdf)
   - Our reading: music is a cost to the words, paid back only if it adds energy or mood the voice can't. A bed under the whole Reel PLUS whooshes on every cut is the worst combo in this study. Our North Star (the viewer repeats the fact at work) is exactly what was measured here: recall.
   - Apply: when a bed is used, cut it out (or drop it hard) for the ~2 s held shot on the key number, and let it come back for the context. That silence is also the sound cue toolkit 5 already asks for. Keep sound effects to the 2-3 structural moments (re-commit, rehook), not every cut.
   - Script cues (fill [ ] with verified facts only): "[music out] Here's the number: [verified number]. [beat] [music back] And here's why it matters to you." / "[silence 0.5 s] Write this date down: [verified date]."
2. Speech or sung words in the background hurt more than steady sound, for everyone; non-native listeners start with less headroom. Scharenborg & van Os's review: "Both groups of listeners seem to be more adversely affected by informational masking [competing speech] than energetic masking [steady noise]"; late second-language listeners need roughly "6 dB" more signal-to-noise to match natives, and "the native advantage can be overcome with SNR adjustments of around +4 to +8 dB." The review concludes the gap comes from language exposure, "not due to a differential effect of background noise" (studies disagree on whether non-natives suffer MORE as noise rises). [Scharenborg & van Os, preprint PDF (TU Delft)](https://pure.tudelft.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/54783441/specom18_preprint.pdf); background: [Lecumberri, Cooke & Cutler 2010 review (MPI)](https://mpi.nl/publications/item_458254)
   - Our reading: a big share of our audience hears English as a second language (Indian viewers, F-1/H-1B workers), often on phone speakers in noisy places. They need the voice clearly above everything else, and a bed with vocals or a talking clip under the voiceover is the worst possible mask.
   - Apply: instrumental beds only, never lyrics or a second voice under the owner's voice. A news clip's original audio plays ONLY in a gap where the owner isn't talking (as evidence, toolkit 15), never under his line. Technical words (OPT, STEM extension, I-140, ticker names) are where a non-native ear fails first, so the bed drops further under those lines and captions spell them right (toolkit 9).
3. Viewers complain about music burying speech, and broadcast practice keeps it far below the voice. BBC survey as reported in 2011: 70% of 8,000 online adults had at least occasional trouble hearing TV speech; among 508 over-65s, 62% said background music "reduced their enjoyment"; the BBC put most problems down to recording rather than the soundtrack. [Hearing Health Matters](https://hearinghealthmatters.org/hearingnewswatch/2011/what-their-telly-tells-them-most-brits-sometimes-cant-tell/). BBC guidance (as quoted by a Salford research record): "turn the background music down by 3 dB when co-present with foreground speech, and avoid lyrics and heavily percussive beats." [Salford repository](https://salford-repository.worktribe.com/output/3636737/effect-of-background-music-arrangement-and-tempo-on-foreground-speech-intelligibility-listening-experiment-data-and-statistical-analyses-spreadsheets). For voice over music, a loudness gap of "at least 10 LU" was suggested (Torcoli et al., cited in Geary et al. 2020); for voice over voice, non-expert listeners aged 57+ preferred 20-30 LU, far more than the mixers' 11.5-17 LU. [Geary et al. 2020, PDF (Salford)](https://salford-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1486952)
   - Our reading: mixers (us) consistently think the bed is quieter than listeners do. Our 11-03 recipe (music to -16 LUFS then -9 dB ≈ -25 LUFS under a -14 LUFS voice, plus sidechain ducking) is about 11 LU apart before ducking: inside the "at least 10 LU" line but near its edge. Avoid "heavily percussive" beds: our "bright" 108 BPM synth bed with drums is the riskier one for voice lines.
   - Apply: aim for ~15 LU between voice and bed while the voice is talking (more under technical words and the key number), let the bed rise only in pauses. Phone check before export: play it on the phone speaker at low volume; if any word is unclear, the bed is too loud.

No contradiction with the toolkit.
